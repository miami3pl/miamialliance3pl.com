Spot Rates Climb While Diesel Slides

FreightWaves reported that dry van spot rates jumped to their highest level since July in early November, even as diesel prices declined. The National Truckload Index (NTI), which FreightWaves describes as measuring all-in dry van spot rate movement across the United States, rose more than 4% since late October. Over the same period, diesel truck stop prices fell roughly 3.5%.

Julie Van de Kamp, reporting on the data as of Tuesday, November 17, said the timing stands out. "It's rare that the NTI starts jumping this early in November ahead of the holidays," she said. FreightWaves noted that this underscores a market behaving differently than seasonal norms would suggest.

For any business that buys truckload capacity, directly or through a logistics provider, the takeaway is simple: a falling fuel price did not translate into cheaper spot freight this month. Budgets built on the assumption that lower diesel means lower trucking costs may need a second look before the holiday stretch.

Why Fuel Is Not Driving This Move

Fuel is often the first explanation shippers reach for when freight costs change. The FreightWaves data points the other way. According to the report, diesel has not seen a sharp increase since early September, yet spot rates have continued to climb throughout that stretch.

Van de Kamp was direct on this point: spot rates are primarily a function of available capacity and supply and demand, not a straight pass-through of fuel costs. FreightWaves said the current data reinforces that view.

The structure of the market helps explain why. FreightWaves noted that the spot market represents only a fraction of overall truckload volume, roughly 10% to 25% of truckload freight depending on market conditions, with the remainder moving under contract. Because the spot pool is relatively small, even a modest surge of freight spilling out of contracted agreements can move spot rates meaningfully.

For shippers, that means watching diesel alone can miss the bigger driver. A load that has to move on short notice, outside a contracted lane, is exposed to whatever capacity looks like that week.

A Late Retailer Inventory Push Sends Freight to the Spot Market

FreightWaves identified a last-minute inventory push by retailers as a significant driver of the rate climb. Van de Kamp pointed to the October Logistics Manager Index reading of 66.9 for inventory level growth, the highest reading since 2022, which Zach Strickland and Dr. Zach Rogers discussed on Freightonomics.

According to the report, many retailers delayed bringing holiday inventory into the country. FreightWaves described that delay as a byproduct of three pressures:

  • Demand uncertainty
  • Rising inventory holding costs
  • More streamlined supply chains

That compressed ordering window is now pushing freight outside of contracted agreements and into the spot market, adding upward pressure on rates, FreightWaves reported. The pattern shows how a decision made upstream, such as waiting to import, can surface later as higher domestic trucking costs.

Tender Rejections Show a Market Still Short of Recovery

Tender rejections, the loads carriers decline when offered freight, are one way to read how tight capacity is. FreightWaves reported that national dry van tender rejection rates have been trending lower since July but remain between 10% and 15%. Rejection rates above 10% are considered a sign of a tight market, and Van de Kamp described the current range as still capacity-constrained.

According to the report, the current level gives carriers some ability to select freight that fits their networks, without the extreme readings seen over the summer. Van de Kamp said the truckload market is "still really not fully recovered" and characterized it as being in an unhealthy state, but one where carriers retain meaningful optionality heading into the holiday stretch.

Carrier optionality matters for shippers. When carriers can choose, freight that fits their networks has an advantage, and loads tendered at the last minute have less leverage on price and timing.

Practical Steps to Limit Spot Market Exposure

The FreightWaves report does not forecast where rates go next, and no single tactic removes freight cost risk. Still, the dynamics it describes suggest a few practical habits for shippers, and businesses that rely on 3PL services in Miami can apply the same lens when planning holiday inventory:

  • Move inventory earlier when possible. The report ties part of the rate climb to a compressed ordering window. Freight that is planned ahead has a better chance of moving under contract instead of spilling into the spot market.
  • Watch capacity signals, not only diesel. Spot rates rose while fuel fell, so tender rejections and spot indexes are worth tracking alongside fuel prices.
  • Position stock close to customers. Inventory stored near end customers can ship in parcel or regional moves rather than last-minute long-haul truckloads.
  • Consolidate inbound freight. Fewer, fuller, planned shipments give carriers freight that is easier to fit into their networks.
  • Keep a buffer for peak. Holding holiday stock in a warehouse before the rush lowers the need to expedite replenishment when capacity is tight.
