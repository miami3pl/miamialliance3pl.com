Ocean Disruption Tests Airfreight Choices for Miami Importers

Cost, aircraft capacity and cargo preparation complicate the decision to move delayed ocean shipments by air.

By Miami Alliance 3PL Team | November 2, 2026 | 7 min read

Ocean shipping disruption is not producing a large-scale move to airfreight, The Loadstar reported, citing DSV. The expense of switching and the practical work required to prepare cargo for flight are keeping conversions limited. For businesses moving inventory through Miami, the report makes a case for reviewing alternatives before a delayed shipment becomes an urgent fulfillment problem.

  • Last-minute airfreight requests face different capacity and cost conditions from contracted cargo.

  • Miami warehouse planning should reflect the receiving and distribution requirements of the chosen transport plan.

A Delayed Ocean Shipment Does Not Automatically Justify Airfreight

According to The Loadstar, DSV head of global airfreight Achim Martinka told TIACA's Air Cargo Forum in Miami that most customers were continuing to use ocean shipping despite disruption. The deciding factor was whether the consequences of a delay justified paying more to move the goods by air. He said switching could be up to 20 times more expensive, while emphasizing that conversion volumes remained limited.

That comparison should be treated as the warning Martinka described, rather than a quote for a particular shipment. For a business owner, the useful starting point is the inventory affected: which products are delayed, which customer commitments depend on them and what remains available to fulfill orders.

Before requesting an alternative, separate urgent goods from inventory that can wait. Ask the freight provider to explain the proposed movement and its cost, then compare that proposal with the business consequences of leaving the cargo at sea. The report supports a shipment-specific decision; disruption alone does not establish that an airfreight switch makes financial sense.

Available Capacity Needs More Than a Last-Minute Inquiry

The Loadstar reported that DSV's airline contracts are generally signed for six or 12 months. Martinka explained that spontaneous requests come at a higher cost than cargo already covered by those arrangements. When the duration of a disruption is unclear, he advised shippers to begin arranging an airfreight solution before available capacity becomes unaffordable.

The same report described demand for dedicated capacity at Atlas Air. Martin Drew, its chief strategy and transformation officer, said forwarders and shippers wanted capacity secured through lengthy contracts. He also said Atlas had demand for dedicated aircraft that it could not currently accommodate. Those comments underline why an inquiry about space should be part of contingency planning.

For an importer or distributor, set a point at which a delay triggers a review with the freight provider. Request options while the business can still compare them. Distinguish a possible routing from a confirmed arrangement, and give the warehouse the status of each. Avoid scheduling receiving or downstream orders around capacity that is still being discussed.

Cargo Must Be Reviewed Before It Moves by Air

Finding aircraft space does not complete the switch. According to The Loadstar, Martinka warned that ocean cargo needs new documentation and operational preparation for air transport. He specifically identified dangerous goods checks, aircraft dimensions and the work needed to rebuild cargo for flight.

Use those points to structure the conversation with the freight provider. Ask what documentation needs to change, whether the goods require a dangerous goods review, whether the shipment fits the proposed aircraft and what physical preparation is necessary. Establish who is responsible for each task before treating the alternative as ready to execute.

For the warehouse receiving the goods, provide the shipment details that follow from the confirmed plan. If the cargo configuration or arrival plan changes, update the receiving instructions accordingly. Specify whether the goods should enter storage, move into order fulfillment or be prepared for onward distribution. Treat transport preparation and warehouse handling as connected planning tasks, with clear instructions for each handoff. This gives the business a concrete way to evaluate the alternative beyond its freight quote.

A Miami Sea-Air Example Shows How Dependencies Add Up

The Loadstar illustrated the difficulty with an account from Emir Pineda, director of air service development at Miami-Dade Aviation Department. He described an effort to move Honduran okra by sea to Miami, then by air through Amsterdam for delivery to the UK. The movement worked once, but he said its complexity prevented a consistent operation.

In that example, the USDA permit imposed specific conditions on the movement. Freighter capacity had to match the ship's arrival, the cargo had to travel together and temperature requirements had to be maintained. These were conditions described for that particular shipment, not a general set of requirements for every import through Miami.

The planning lesson is to evaluate the whole proposed route. Ask which conditions apply to the goods, which approvals or documents the route requires and which connections depend on a precise arrival. For a business distributing between Latin America and the United States, a route that appears workable on paper still needs a review of its individual handoffs. Do not assume that a successful movement establishes a repeatable schedule.

Turn a Backup Route Into a Usable Inventory Plan

According to The Loadstar, Martinka said most shippers had developed some form of emergency plan since Covid. However, he was not seeing a regular shift of ocean volumes into airfreight simply to keep a contingency route active. He suggested that an airfreight option could form part of a resilience plan that is tested periodically.

Apply that idea by reviewing a backup plan against actual products and customer commitments. Select the inventory that would warrant an alternative, identify who can approve the expense and ask the freight provider what information is needed to assess capacity. Include the warehouse receiving plan in that review.

  • Identify the goods and orders affected by the ocean delay.
  • Request a transport proposal with its preparation requirements.
  • Confirm the status of capacity and the expected arrival arrangement.
  • Provide receiving, storage and outbound instructions to the warehouse.
  • Revisit the decision when the shipment's circumstances change.

Keep the review focused on decisions the business can make. The objective is to understand when an alternative is justified and what must happen before the goods can follow it.
