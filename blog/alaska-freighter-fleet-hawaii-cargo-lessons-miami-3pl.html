Alaska Doubles Freighter Fleet: Air Cargo Lessons for Miami 3PL Users

Alaska Air Group is adding four converted freighters to target Hawaii cargo demand, and the real test is connecting that capacity to customers.

By Miami Alliance 3PL Team | November 28, 2026 | 8 min read

Alaska Air Group is doubling its dedicated freighter fleet by adding four Boeing 737-800 converted freighters in the first half of next year, The Loadstar reported. The move targets cargo demand in Hawaii and a larger international freight business. The publication also noted that the harder task may be turning the new aircraft capacity into lasting revenue. For importers, exporters and online sellers, the story shows how air freight capacity is built, sold and connected to the ground.

What Alaska Air Group Announced

The Loadstar reported that Alaska Air Group plans to introduce four Boeing 737-800 converted freighters in the first half of next year. The additions take its dedicated narrowbody fleet from five aircraft to nine, which doubles the group's dedicated freighter capacity.

Two of the new aircraft are earmarked for Alaska Airlines and two for its sister carrier, Hawaiian Airlines. According to The Loadstar, the group can still allocate capacity across the network according to demand and operational needs. The split is a starting point, not a fixed assignment.

Each aircraft can carry approximately 50,000lb of cargo. The publication said this gives the carriers greater scope to develop regional freight operations while connecting shipments with the group's expanding international network.

Why Hawaii Was the Focus

Alaska describes the investment as a response to an existing capacity constraint in Hawaii. Hawaiian Airlines has served the market for nearly 85 years. Ian Morgan, VP cargo at Alaska Airlines, told The Loadstar that the carrier's reliance on passenger aircraft has limited its ability to meet the full potential of the cargo market.

"By adding dedicated freighter service and leveraging Hawaiian's brand, we can meet that demand while empowering a more interconnected, global network," Morgan told the publication.

The new aircraft in Hawaii will support shipments between the islands. They will also add capacity for seafood, agricultural produce and other locally manufactured goods. Morgan said the combined operations would help support local industries, especially those that need reliable, palletized transport. He added, "The freighter network will connect seamlessly with our widebody fleet," which links the regional freighters to the group's larger aircraft.

The Loadstar also pointed to the geography behind the opportunity. Hawaii's island communities are dispersed. Dedicated aircraft could provide greater consistency for larger shipments, while passenger aircraft continue to offer additional belly capacity.

Capacity Is Not the Same as Revenue

The Loadstar stated the central question directly: the challenge might be turning additional aircraft capacity into sustainable revenue growth. More planes do not automatically bring more cargo. The publication said the risk is that new capacity simply shifts existing volumes between more aircraft instead of attracting new business.

One commercial advantage is the ability to move shipments under a single air waybill. That could give Hawaiian cargo customers greater access to international markets. The Loadstar added that the benefits will depend on three things:

  • The availability of onward capacity beyond the first leg.
  • Competitive transit times from origin to final destination.
  • The ability to attract enough freight volume to keep the aircraft full.

The report concluded that Alaska's success may depend on how effectively it connects regional markets to its expanding international network. A freighter's value depends on what it connects to at both ends of the trip.

Cargo as a Revenue Diversification Strategy

The expansion is part of a broader effort. According to The Loadstar, Alaska Air Group's Alaska Accelerate strategy identifies cargo as an important source of revenue diversification. Morgan said the group's growth opportunities span cargo services in Alaska and Hawaii, the continental US, international operations, ACMI leasing and other ancillary engagements.

The group has set a target of growing annual cargo revenue to $750m by 2030. It has increased cargo revenue by approximately 60% since 2024, according to its September investor day presentation as cited by The Loadstar. Other opportunities it named include mail, international belly capacity and broader network connectivity.

ACMI leasing means providing aircraft, crews, maintenance and insurance to third-party operators. It could let Alaska earn additional returns from its freighter assets. The Loadstar noted that the airline has not said how much of its future fleet capacity might go to such arrangements. Morgan said the goal was to reach new markets while keeping existing services reliable. He said doubling dedicated capacity gives the group "more flexibility to allocate aircraft where they are needed" across its network.

What Shippers Can Take From the Story

This is a Hawaii story, but its lessons apply to any business that imports, exports or sells online.

  • Dedicated and belly capacity work differently. The Loadstar described dedicated freighters as offering greater consistency for larger shipments, with passenger belly space as an extra layer. Shippers moving pallets rather than parcels may want to ask which type of capacity their forwarder is booking.
  • The connection matters as much as the flight. A single air waybill and an international network help only if onward capacity and transit times hold up. The same applies on the ground. The handoff from airport to warehouse to customer is part of the total transit time.
  • Capacity follows demand. Alaska kept the flexibility to move aircraft to where demand is. Shippers can plan the same way and keep inventory positioned close to their customers and carriers.
  • Palletized freight needs a receiving plan. Morgan singled out industries that need reliable, palletized transport. Freight that arrives on pallets needs a warehouse ready to receive it, store it and break it down.
