What the Port of Savannah Reported for October

FreightWaves reported that the Port of Savannah handled 565,366 TEUs in October, up 25% from a year earlier and the second-highest monthly container total in the port's history. Savannah handled 160 vessels during the month, up 5% year over year.

The Georgia Ports Authority (GPA) said the October gains pushed container volumes for fiscal 2027 to date up 7.8%, an increase of 152,991 TEUs compared with the same period a year earlier. October followed a 3.7% year-over-year gain in September, when Savannah handled 504,015 TEUs amid rising imports and weather-related shipment delays.

In its own summary of why the story matters, FreightWaves said the increase means more containers moving through a Southeast U.S. gateway used by importers, exporters, trucking companies and railroads during the holiday shipping period.

What Drove the Increase

FreightWaves reported that GPA Chief Executive Griff Lynch said in a statement that container volumes at the global top 30 gateway were driven by strong consumer spending and an extended peak shipping season.

Routing changes are also part of the picture. According to FreightWaves, the return of some ocean carriers to Suez Canal routing is cutting transit times to Savannah by 10 to 12 days as shippers replenish inventory during the holiday shopping season.

For an importer, a shorter voyage is usually welcome. It also means a container can reach port earlier than a receiving plan built on older transit times assumed, which puts pressure on the warehouse side of the supply chain to be ready sooner.

Savannah and Brunswick Capacity Projects

The cargo gains come as Georgia Ports advances projects designed to expand Savannah's capacity and improve connections to inland markets. In October, the authority announced a $2.3-billion Savannah Container Terminal investment after receiving a permit from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. FreightWaves reported that the planned 350-acre facility would provide 3.5 million TEUs of annual capacity, on-dock rail and direct highway access for trucks. Construction is scheduled to begin in 2028, and the authority said the completed project would bring Savannah's total annual capacity to 12 million TEUs.

Separately, the $1.6-billion Ocean Terminal redevelopment is more than 60% complete and will raise that facility's annual container capacity from 200,000 TEUs to 1.75 million TEUs. GPA said truck gate construction and support services are 99% complete, and FreightWaves previously reported that the project's first container-yard phase is scheduled for completion in mid-fall 2027, with full yard completion targeted for the end of 2028.

Rail is part of the plan as well. The Gainesville Inland Port provides another connection to Savannah, with Norfolk Southern operating direct rail service five days a week between the port and Northeast Georgia. Georgia Ports expects the facility to shift 26,000 containers from highways to rail during its first year.

At the Port of Brunswick, which FreightWaves described as the leading East Coast gateway for vehicle imports, total roll-on/roll-off cargo fell 3.6% year over year to 69,599 units in October. Heavy machinery shipments rose 25.8% to 5,193 units, compared with 4,127 a year earlier. A fourth berth at Brunswick's Colonels Island facility is 60% complete and scheduled to open in November 2027, according to GPA.

Planning Lessons for Importers and Online Sellers

None of these figures come from South Florida, but the conditions behind them, heavy holiday imports, a long peak season and changing transit times, are worth weighing for any business that buys 3PL services in Miami. A few practical lessons follow from the details FreightWaves reported.

  • Arrival dates can move up. When a routing change cuts transit time, a container can reach port days earlier than an older schedule showed. Confirming estimated arrival dates with the freight forwarder as the vessel gets closer helps the warehouse book unloading labor and space for the right week.
  • Peak season can run longer. The GPA chief executive pointed to an extended peak shipping season. Businesses that planned storage around a short holiday window may need room for inventory that keeps arriving later, and for the returns that follow the selling season.
  • Receiving and outbound are linked. Inventory that arrives sooner only helps if it can be unloaded, counted and made available to ship. For goods headed straight to a retailer or a marketplace, cross-docking can shorten the time product spends sitting on the warehouse floor.
  • Clearance is a separate step. Keeping the licensed customs broker and the warehouse in contact helps the warehouse know when goods are released and ready to be picked up.
  • Long-term capacity is long-term. Construction of the new Savannah Container Terminal is scheduled to begin in 2028, and the Ocean Terminal yard phases run into 2027 and 2028. Those projects matter for future routing decisions, but this season's inventory moves through the capacity that exists today.

The Panama Canal Detail for Latin America Trade

The authority also cited improving Panama Canal conditions. According to FreightWaves, the canal authority increased daily vessel transits from 33 to 34 effective Nov. 12 and raised the maximum allowable draft at the Neopanamax locks from 49 feet to 50 feet.

The report does not say how those changes affect any particular trade lane, so they are best treated as one input in conversations with forwarders and carriers, not as a schedule commitment. For brands that sell in both the United States and Latin America from one inventory position, canal conditions are one more reason to watch ocean schedules closely.
