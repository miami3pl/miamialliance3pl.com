SONAR data points to a tighter holiday trucking market, and South Florida shippers can plan around it with earlier inventory moves and clear order timing.

What the SONAR Data Shows Before Thanksgiving

FreightWaves reported that truckload tender rejection rates jumped back toward 13% in the days leading up to Thanksgiving. Zach Strickland of SONAR is forecasting a further climb to roughly 14% over the following week as holiday capacity pressures intensify. The summary at the top of the FreightWaves piece went a step further, saying the jump could potentially push rates over 14% as low inventory levels drive last-minute shipping demands.

The number matters well beyond trucking companies. According to FreightWaves, rejection rates signal how much pricing power carriers hold and how difficult it will be to cover loads at contracted rates. When the rate rises, carriers are turning down more of the freight offered to them, and the businesses behind that freight feel it in price and in timing.

Strickland said demand signals, as measured by the tender volume index, remain underwhelming heading into the holiday, and he added that this is not unusual. In his telling, peak season is about supply more than demand. In his words, "We call it peak season because capacity gets tighter" and the reasons are mostly about people. He said the market loses a lot of drivers, that drivers get "boundaried around 500 miles" and that larger fleets have to convince drivers to get on the road during the holidays, when they want to be home with their families. His summary of the cost side was short: "a premium comes with that".

Two Pressures That Could Push Rates Above Seasonal Norms

FreightWaves said two compounding factors could push rejection rates and spot rates higher than seasonal norms this year.

Lean inventory going into the holiday

Strickland noted that inventory levels heading into the holiday are not as robust as typical, which raises the risk of last-minute shipping activity. Businesses that did not move goods earlier may need to move them quickly once orders arrive, and that late freight competes for the same thinner pool of holiday drivers.

A potential port strike

A potential port strike was also cited as a source of shipper anxiety over freight positioning. Strickland said a resolved strike would likely produce only a modest bump, with more meaningful rate movement expected closer to Christmas. The FreightWaves summary also warned that, coupled with potential port strikes, capacity could become extremely constrained in certain regions.

For planning, the practical point is timing. The report describes pressure in the stretch right after Thanksgiving and a later, larger push as Christmas approaches.

Spot Rates Are Climbing Without Help From Fuel

On the spot side, FreightWaves reported that Strickland was more emphatic about upside risk. The van spot rate index, along with refrigerated and flatbed rates, have already seen some elevation this month. Diesel prices are declining, so fuel is not the driver.

Strickland put it plainly: "This is not a fuel-related situation". He said the market is seeing actual elevation, and already elevated rates, on all three modes. He added that ad hoc and transactional freight hitting the spot market in the coming weeks makes a downward trend unlikely, and that a decline would be noteworthy if it occurred.

Businesses that lean on spot coverage for overflow or rush freight can treat this as a budgeting signal. An earlier article on this blog looks at the same gap between rising rates and falling diesel: Truckload Spot Rates Rise as Diesel Falls: Miami 3PL Takeaways.

Regional Hotspots Named in the Report

FreightWaves reported that the weighted rejection index, which accounts for volume, is flashing week-over-week increases in several places:

  • The Midwestern corridor, including Iowa, Kansas and Topeka.
  • Phoenix.
  • Gulf Coast markets such as New Orleans and Mobile.

Because these are lower-volume markets, the report said, the signal on the weighted rejection index map carries outsized significance for capacity availability.

The Pacific Northwest is another area to watch. Strickland pointed to heavy freight inflows at the ports of Seattle and Tacoma from shippers diverting cargo to avoid Los Angeles congestion, combined with atmospheric rivers hitting the West Coast and snow in the Rockies and Cascades. Low freight density in mountain and prairie regions makes loads harder to cover, adding to rate pressure in those lanes. Temperature-controlled freight rounds out the concern: FreightWaves reported refrigerated rates elevated on the West Coast, even though the reefer market's true seasonal peak typically arrives in January.

Miami was not among the markets the report named. For South Florida businesses, the national points carry more weight: thin inventories, elevated spot rates across modes and fewer drivers on the road over the holidays. A shipment that leaves Miami can still cross lanes where capacity is tight, so the regional picture is worth checking against where customers actually are.

Planning Steps Before the Holiday Push

The report does not call for panic. It calls for moving decisions earlier. Practical steps for the weeks ahead:

  1. Position inventory before it is needed. The report ties part of the risk to lean stock and last-minute shipping. Goods already sitting in a warehouse close to customers or to the port do not need a rush truckload when orders spike.
  2. Separate urgent freight from flexible freight. Spot exposure is easier to manage when only the truly time-sensitive loads compete for scarce capacity.
  3. Watch the calendar, not only the rate. Strickland expects more meaningful rate movement closer to Christmas, so loads that can move sooner may avoid the later squeeze.
  4. Plan inbound containers with the port situation in mind. Shipper anxiety over freight positioning is part of the current picture, so it helps to know where inbound goods will be received and how quickly they can be unloaded.
  5. Confirm order cutoffs with the fulfillment provider. Clear cutoff times help customer service teams set accurate delivery expectations in the busiest weeks.
