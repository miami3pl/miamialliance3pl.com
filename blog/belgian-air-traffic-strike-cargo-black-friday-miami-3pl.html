Belgian Air Traffic Strike Hits Peak Cargo: Lessons for Miami 3PL Users

The Loadstar reported some 70 flights affected at Liège as airlines rerouted freighters, a reminder of how quickly peak-season air cargo can slip off schedule.

What Happened at Liège and Brussels

The Loadstar reported that airlines rescheduled flights and moved aircraft to airports in neighboring countries after a Belgian air traffic control strike disrupted cargo operations at Liège and Brussels, four days before Black Friday. Liège Airport spokesperson Christian Delcourt told the publication that some 70 flights were affected. At the time of the report, the airport had not said how many of those flights were canceled, retimed or rerouted, or when it expected affected freight to move.

Flight-tracking data cited by The Loadstar showed Emirates SkyCargo flight EK9989 from Dubai, originally due at Liège, rerouted to Copenhagen. Maastricht Aachen Airport said it expected to take two cargo flights diverted because of the strike, without naming the airlines or the airports those flights were originally meant to serve.

Brussels Airport canceled all arrivals and departures scheduled between 7am and 9pm local time, posted the same notice on its cargo arrivals and departures pages, and advised customers with later flights to check with their airlines. According to The Loadstar, the airport had not published a freighter tally or a timetable for normal cargo operations to resume.

The walkout was part of a national protest against government austerity measures. The Board of Airline Representatives in Belgium said it was the 11th strike to affect Belgian airspace in 11 months. Brussels Airlines said it only learned late on Saturday afternoon that controllers would take part, which left little time to rework the Monday schedule.

Couriers, Mail and Road Freight Feel the Effects

The disruption did not stop at the two largest airports. The Loadstar reported that Charleroi canceled flights for the whole day, while Ostend-Bruges was working with airlines to reschedule flights, including three cargo departures and two arrivals. During an earlier stoppage on June 2, local broadcaster Qu4tre reported that 35 to 40 flights were affected at Liège, with some diverted to Cologne. This time, Liège had not yet identified the airports taking rerouted flights.

Parcel and mail networks issued their own warnings. FedEx warned of possible delays to shipments to, from and within Belgium. Jersey Post Global Logistics warned that disruption at Brussels Airport could delay inbound international mail and parcels, and that the wider strike could affect collections, processing and deliveries across the country.

Chronopost pointed to the ground side of the problem. It warned that expected blockages on major roads and in industrial areas could lengthen transit times for shipments to and from Belgium throughout Black Friday week. It said all of its Belgian sites would remain open, although collections and deliveries would be disrupted. For a seller, that detail matters: a strike aimed at air traffic control can still slow trucks, pickups and final delivery on the ground.

Why Capacity Recovers Slower Than Flights Resume

The Loadstar connected the Belgian strike to the recent U.S. air traffic control outage as an example of how long capacity can take to recover. The FAA lifted its nationwide ground stop on November 13, after less than five hours. Even so, capacity on international flights out of the U.S. was still about 10% below the previous week's level over the weekend, according to WorldACD.

Expeditors warned that recovery would vary by airline and airport as carriers repositioned aircraft and crews. That is the part of a disruption that shippers can easily underestimate. A stoppage can end within a day, but aircraft, crews and the freight that piled up in the meantime need time to get back into place, and peak-season demand leaves little spare room to absorb a backlog.

A Busy Cargo Hub Already Adjusting to Lower Ecommerce Volume

The timing added pressure. The Loadstar noted that the strike falls in "one of Liège's busiest periods for freight", at a moment when the airport is adjusting to a sharp drop in ecommerce traffic. Liège said small-parcel volumes had fallen 42% after the EU's €3 duty took effect on July 1, reducing the number of dedicated ecommerce flights. Aircraft movements fell 6% year on year in the third quarter, while freight tonnage was virtually flat at 334,467 tonnes.

Exports have helped. Stronger exports lifted total throughput 7.3% year on year, to 1.03m tonnes in the first nine months, and Liège singled out pharmaceuticals, data center equipment and flowers as supporting July volumes. Last month, according to The Loadstar, the airport also signed an agreement with Brazil's Viracopos Airport to attract freighter services between Belgium and Brazil, a sign that Liège is working to build cargo links with Latin America.

For U.S. and Latin American sellers who source from or ship through Europe, the takeaway is that duty changes, labor actions and airport capacity can all shift at the same time. For more on the European side of the peak, see the earlier analysis of EU parcel charges and Black Friday bundles.

Peak-Season Planning Lessons for Importers and Online Sellers

No importer controls a Belgian air traffic control roster, but every importer controls how much margin it builds into its own plan. For online sellers and distributors, including those that rely on 3PL services in Miami, the Belgian strike points to a few practical habits for the weeks around Black Friday:

  • Move holiday stock early. Inventory that has already landed and been received in a U.S. warehouse is no longer waiting on a freighter slot in Europe.
  • Plan for the recovery, not just the event. As the WorldACD figures showed, capacity can stay below normal for days after flights resume.
  • Ask suppliers and forwarders about alternate routings. Airlines in this case shifted flights to airports in neighboring countries; knowing in advance which alternatives a forwarder uses helps set realistic arrival dates.
  • Watch the ground leg too. Chronopost's warning about road blockages shows that a strike can slow pickups and deliveries even when sites stay open.
  • Keep customs paperwork ready. When freight arrives late and all at once, clean documents give a licensed customs broker one less reason for a delay.
