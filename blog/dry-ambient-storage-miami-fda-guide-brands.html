Dry and Ambient Storage in Miami: FDA Guidance for Brands

What shelf-stable food brands should understand about storage instructions, food safety responsibilities, and preparing inventory for a Miami warehouse.

Before arranging dry or ambient storage for shelf-stable food, confirm the product's storage directions and which food safety requirements apply to the operation handling it. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) publishes consumer food-storage guidance and a separate Preventive Controls for Human Food rule that addresses covered food facilities. Start with the product's storage directions

FDA's consumer guide, Are You Storing Food Safely?, advises readers to check storage directions on labels. It also warns that some foods beyond the obvious perishable categories require refrigeration. For a brand planning dry storage, the practical starting point is therefore the product's instructions, rather than an assumption based on its packaging or sales category.

Prepare a product list with the storage directions for each item. Ask the manufacturer to clarify unclear instructions before assigning that item to ambient storage. Include any stated temperature or other storage conditions in the information you give the warehouse.

The supplied FDA references do not establish a universal temperature or humidity range for all dry goods. Avoid treating ambient storage as a single specification that automatically fits every product. For non-food goods, provide their own handling instructions; these FDA food references should not be treated as a storage rulebook for unrelated merchandise.

Turn FDA's storage advice into receiving instructions

FDA advises checking canned goods for swelling, leakage, punctures, holes, fractures, extensive deep rusting, and severe crushing or denting. The agency also notes that stickiness on a can's exterior may indicate a leak. These details give food brands a useful starting point for discussing what a receiving inspection should flag.

Prepare written instructions for reporting suspect packaging and deciding what happens to affected stock. Identify who should review the condition, what information they need, and who can authorize the next action. Treat this as a proposed operating procedure to agree with the warehouse, rather than assuming every inspection is included in a storage arrangement.

FDA also advises keeping non-perishable foods away from household cleaning products and chemicals. If a shipment contains food alongside other merchandise, identify the products and request a storage review before arrival. Finally, FDA warns that food can be unsafe even without an unusual appearance or smell. A visual inspection should not become the sole basis for deciding that questionable food is safe.

Separate consumer guidance from facility requirements

FDA's food-storage guide offers consumer advice. Its Preventive Controls for Human Food rule addresses commercial food operations. According to FDA, domestic and foreign food facilities required to register generally must comply with the rule's risk-based preventive controls and modernized Current Good Manufacturing Practices, unless an exemption applies. FDA specifically states that the applicability of those manufacturing practices does not depend on whether a facility must register.

For a business buying storage, the action is to establish which requirements apply to the relevant facility and activities. Do not assume that a general description such as dry storage settles that determination. Review applicable exemptions or modified requirements before deciding what documentation is needed.

FDA says covered facilities must have and implement a written food safety plan. The hazard analysis must consider known or reasonably foreseeable biological, chemical, and physical hazards. When that analysis identifies a hazard requiring a preventive control, the facility must implement a written control for it. Ask how the proposed handling activities fit the applicable plan and who is responsible for each relevant task.

Discuss controls, training, and records

FDA describes food allergen controls as written procedures addressing allergen cross-contact and appropriate allergen labeling. It describes sanitation controls as procedures and practices that maintain sanitary conditions to minimize or prevent relevant hazards. Which preventive controls are necessary follows from the hazard analysis; avoid turning every example in the agency's overview into a universal warehouse requirement.

According to FDA, monitoring must be appropriate to the preventive control and documented. Corrective actions include addressing a problem, reducing the likelihood of recurrence, evaluating affected food, and preventing its entry into commerce when its safety cannot be ensured. Verification activities and corrective actions also require records.

FDA further requires management to ensure that employees who manufacture, process, pack, or hold food are qualified for their duties and receive appropriate food hygiene and safety training. During storage planning, discuss the instructions relevant to your products and the records needed for applicable controls. If the hazard analysis identifies a hazard requiring a preventive control, FDA also requires a written recall plan.

Prepare the storage handoff before inventory arrives

Use the FDA references to build a practical discussion packet. The following is a preparation checklist for your business, rather than a list of documents FDA universally requires every storage customer to submit:

  • Product instructions: Label copies, manufacturer storage directions, and clarification of any conditions that remain unclear.
  • Inventory descriptions: A clear distinction between food and other goods, plus information relevant to chemical separation and allergen handling.
  • Packaging review: Conditions to report on arrival and instructions for escalating damaged or questionable stock.
  • Responsibilities: Contacts authorized to make inventory decisions and the division of tasks under applicable food safety procedures.
  • Records and recall procedures: Documentation needed for applicable controls and instructions relevant to the facility's recall plan, where required.

Discuss package dates as well. FDA explains that a manufacturer's use-by date generally concerns best flavor or quality, rather than food safety, while identifying exceptions for infant formula and some baby foods. Prepare explicit date-handling instructions instead of treating every package date as having the same meaning.
