Understand what CBP agriculture specialists look for

According to U.S. Customs and Border Protection, invasive species and toxic substances threaten American agriculture and natural resources. Its agriculture specialists use targeting, detection and interception to help prevent those threats from entering the country. For importers, the practical point is to prepare the shipment with agricultural risks in mind, including the materials surrounding the goods.

CBP specifically asks importers to follow practices that keep cargo and conveyances free from dangerous species and substances. Discuss that expectation with the supplier and transportation team before loading. Ask how they will check the goods, packaging and loading area, and identify who will respond if a concern appears.

For produce, flowers and other agricultural inventory, prepare a precise product description for the client's licensed customs broker. Use that discussion to establish what product-specific requirements need further verification. CBP's overview explains its protective mission; it does not establish that a particular shipment is eligible for entry.

Include pallets and packing materials in shipment preparation

CBP warns that wood packaging material can conceal larvae of wood-boring insects capable of attacking native trees or nursery stock. That concern applies to the packaging around imported goods, so preparation should extend beyond the product itself.

Before dispatch, ask the supplier to describe the pallets, crates and other packing materials being used. Request a review of their condition and a contact who can answer packaging questions. Keep that information with the shipment description so it is available to the people coordinating the import.

  • Identify the packaging materials before loading.
  • Ask the supplier to flag visible insects or unfamiliar material for review.
  • Keep packaging questions connected to the shipment and broker contact.
  • Resolve uncertainties before making firm warehouse receiving arrangements.

These are preparation steps based on CBP's stated concerns. They do not replace an agency inspection or determine the outcome of one.

Treat FDA food prior notice as a separate preparation task

According to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, FDA must receive prior notification of food imported or offered for import into the United States, including animal feed. FDA explains that advance notice helps it target import inspections with CBP's support and protect the food supply against public health threats.

FDA also says the person submitting prior notice must report the name of any country that refused entry to the food. If a shipment has such a history, bring that information into the filing discussion early. Do not leave the person preparing the submission to reconstruct it during the receiving handoff.

Assign responsibility for preparing and checking prior notice, and confirm the applicable submission timing before shipping. The supplied FDA overview does not give a general filing deadline for every transportation method, so it should not be used to invent one.

Keep the scope clear when discussing flowers or other nonfood agricultural goods. FDA describes a food prior notice requirement. An agricultural product description alone does not establish whether that food requirement applies; clarify the product's intended use and applicable requirements with the broker.

Translate product needs into written storage instructions

For temperature-sensitive inventory, prepare a storage brief that describes the actual conditions required. Avoid relying on a broad label such as produce or agricultural goods. Ask the supplier to provide the handling instructions for the specific product, then review those instructions with the warehouse team before booking receiving.

The brief should state the required temperature conditions, ventilation needs, packaging configuration and intended storage duration. Where the supplier specifies limits or handling precautions, carry those instructions into the receiving plan. Ask the storage provider to confirm suitability for the inventory before dispatch.

  • Describe the product and its packaging clearly.
  • Provide supplier instructions for temperature and airflow.
  • Explain how the goods should be handled on arrival.
  • Name the person authorized to resolve a condition concern.
  • Outline the expected next destination and dispatch plan.

These are operational recommendations for selecting storage and preparing a handoff. The CBP and FDA reference pages do not prescribe warehouse temperature settings or ventilation specifications for the products in your shipment.

Connect border preparation with warehouse receiving

Keep the broker, transportation contact and warehouse team working from the same shipment description. Before arranging receiving, confirm who will communicate clearance status, who will update arrival plans and who can answer questions about the goods. Ask how changes in the import schedule should be communicated to the storage team.

Prepare a receiving packet containing the product description, packaging details, storage instructions and relevant shipment references. Include the prior notice information where applicable, along with any entry-refusal history that must be addressed in the FDA submission. Agree on how the warehouse should escalate differences between the expected inventory and what arrives.
