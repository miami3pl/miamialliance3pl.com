Shein Revenue Slides 6% on EU Fees: Lessons for Miami 3PL Users

New EU parcel fees and an FTC settlement are pushing the fast-fashion retailer toward local inventory, and sellers who ship from Miami can learn from both.

By Miami Alliance 3PL Team | November 26, 2026 | 10 min read

Shein said on Wednesday that its European business could take a year to recover from new EU e-commerce import fees, and FreightWaves reported that the retailer's third-quarter net revenue fell 6% to $10.27 billion. Shein also agreed to pay $100 million to settle U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) claims that it misled consumers about shipping times and products. Both stories point to the same question for online sellers: where inventory sits, and which delivery times a business can actually support.

What Shein Reported for the Third Quarter

FreightWaves reported that Shein Global Holdings posted adjusted net income of $790 million for the third quarter ended Sept. 30, up 27% year over year, while net revenue declined 6% to $10.27 billion. According to FreightWaves, profit rose despite the lower revenue because the company cut costs.

Europe was the weakest region for the newly listed online retailer. Revenue in Europe fell 27.6% to $2.98 billion in the quarter after Shein raised prices to offset new European Union fees on low-value parcels. FreightWaves also pointed to a 9.4% revenue decline in the United States, which last year hiked tariffs on small-dollar parcel imports.

Shein said soft demand in Europe could take a year to recover. CEO Sky Xu told analysts during an earnings briefing that European sales likely would not bounce back until the second half of 2027. Xu also said the company is putting more resources into Latin America and other regions to reduce its dependence on Europe and North America.

How the EU Parcel Fees Work

According to FreightWaves, the European Union implemented a 3 euro ($3.36) fee on low-value packages entering the bloc in July, and on Nov. 1 it imposed an additional 2 euro ($2.24) parcel handling charge. The fees apply to each different product category within a package. FreightWaves gave the example of a shipment with four distinct items, which could be subject to customs fees of nearly $22.

The new rules eliminate duty-free status on so-called de minimis shipments. FreightWaves reported that they are designed to slow entry of low-cost, subsidized and unethically sourced Chinese imports that undercut domestic manufacturers and retailers and that also provide a conduit for drug smuggling.

The practical effect is the part logistics buyers should study. FreightWaves reported that the change significantly reduced the competitive advantage of China-based marketplaces and stores like Shein, forcing them to set up local fulfillment warehouses and ship goods in bulk through traditional ocean and air freight channels rather than directly to consumers from overseas. In plain terms, a model built on single parcels crossing a border one order at a time is being pushed toward a model built on inventory that sits in the market where the buyer lives.

Because the fees are counted per product category, mixed orders and bundles are affected most. For more on that angle, see EU Parcel Charges Test Black Friday Bundles: Lessons for Miami 3PL Users.

Why Local Inventory Is Carrying More of Shein's Deliveries

FreightWaves reported that Shein's local fulfillment program in Europe, which the company calls Flash Forward-Deployed Inventory, handles more than 40% of deliveries to European customers. Shein expects that share to climb to about 50% by the end of the year.

The company said products fulfilled through the program have higher conversion rates and generate more profit per item than products shipped cross-border. That is a notable statement from a company that FreightWaves described as being forced away from shipping directly to consumers from overseas. By Shein's own account, stock held near the customer is performing better at the order level, not only at the border.

Smaller sellers face the same math on a different scale. A business that ships every order as a single parcel across a border carries parcel-level fees and tariffs on each order. The alternative FreightWaves described, moving goods in bulk through ocean and air freight channels into local fulfillment warehouses, changes where and how those costs land. Businesses comparing 3PL services in Miami can run both models for their own products and see which one gives a better landed cost per order and a shorter delivery window for their customers.

The FTC Settlement and the Cost of Missed Delivery Times

Shein also agreed to pay $100 million to settle claims by the FTC, which announced the settlement on Tuesday, FreightWaves reported. The FTC alleged that the company made it difficult to resolve purchase issues, failed to deliver on its shipping commitments, suppressed negative reviews and made deceptive supply chain claims, including about the origin of cotton products.

The shipping allegations are the ones most relevant to logistics. According to FreightWaves, the FTC alleged in a complaint that Shein marketed fast, transparent and reliable shipping, but tens of thousands of consumers reported that their orders arrived days, weeks or months late, while some never received their orders at all. The FTC also alleged that Shein did not provide adequate shipping updates, including legally required delay notices.

FreightWaves reported that Shein ships nearly one million packages to the United States each day but employed only five customer service agents as of September 2025 to handle telephone inquiries from American consumers, and did not provide a phone number for those agents on its website. Under the settlement, Shein agreed not to misrepresent shipping and delivery times, not to suppress negative product reviews, and not to make deceptive claims about supplier and manufacturer audits or product traceability, while also increasing its customer service workforce.

In a statement, Shein said it had reached an agreement to resolve the investigation, which it described as related to certain historical business practices. The company said it disagrees with the FTC's characterizations but is taking steps to bolster its compliance practices.

Compliance Lessons for Sellers of Any Size

FreightWaves reported that Shein is also hosting a series of compliance sessions with third-party experts to educate marketplace sellers on EU chemical safety regulations, compliance requirements for high-risk substances in consumer products, and safety and compliance requirements for lithium batteries in consumer electronics. The sessions are designed to help small businesses understand and comply with regulatory requirements for products sold into the EU.

Taken together, the story points to a few operational habits that apply well beyond fast fashion:

  • Advertise only the delivery times the operation can support. The FTC allegations centered on shipping times that did not match what was marketed and on missing delay notices. Delivery windows should come from real order cutoffs, carrier transit times and warehouse performance, not from marketing goals.
  • Keep records that back up every product claim. According to FreightWaves, the FTC alleged that cotton Shein represented as U.S.-grown was found to originate from China, and that Shein failed to provide a single audit supporting its supplier claims. Origin and sourcing statements on a listing need documents behind them.
  • Know which products carry special handling rules. Lithium batteries and chemical substances were the focus of Shein's seller sessions. Sellers of electronics or products with regulated chemicals should confirm handling requirements before inventory reaches a warehouse.
  • Make it easy for customers to reach someone. FreightWaves reported that consumer requests to Shein often went unanswered, according to the FTC. Clear contact channels and a working returns process are part of fulfillment, not an afterthought.
