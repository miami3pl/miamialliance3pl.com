Secure Storage in Miami: CTPAT Criteria for 3PL Buyers

A practical guide to using CBP's security criteria when evaluating warehouse services for electronics, gaming products and luxury inventory.

By Miami Alliance 3PL Team | October 17, 2026 | 6 min read

Before choosing storage for high-value goods, ask how the warehouse controls access, protects loaded containers and handles signs of tampering. U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) publishes the Customs Trade Partnership Against Terrorism (CTPAT) program requirements and security criteria for third-party logistics providers. Those materials give buyers a framework for evaluating security procedures, while distinguishing voluntary program participation from the services a warehouse offers.

Key Takeaways

  • According to CBP, CTPAT participation is voluntary; its criteria should not be presented as a universal warehouse licensing requirement.
  • CBP's criteria call for secure container and trailer storage areas and procedures addressing unauthorized entry.
  • For applicable shipments, CBP specifies high-security seals, written seal procedures and reporting of compromised seals or containers.
  • Ask prospective warehouses for documented responsibilities, inspection procedures and an escalation process for inventory discrepancies.

Understand what CTPAT participation means

According to CBP, companies joining CTPAT agree to work with the agency to protect the supply chain, identify security gaps and implement security measures. Participation is voluntary. Applicants must review the minimum security criteria for their business category and submit a supply chain security profile explaining how they meet those criteria, following a risk assessment.

CBP's third-party logistics criteria focus eligibility on providers directly involved in handling and managing cargo in the international supply chain, from loading at origin through the first U.S. port of arrival. The document excludes entities providing only domestic services from that enrollment category.

For a warehouse buyer, keep two inquiries separate: what security procedures apply to your inventory, and what evidence supports any claimed CTPAT status. A secure-storage service description alone does not establish program participation. Ask the provider to explain which operations, shipment stages and responsibilities its security documentation covers.

Start with storage areas and access control

CBP's criteria require containers and trailers to be stored in secure areas that prevent unauthorized access or manipulation. They also require procedures for reporting and neutralizing unauthorized entry into containers, trailers or their storage areas. These provisions address physical protection and the response when that protection is breached.

Use those requirements to start a practical warehouse review. For high-value inventory, ask the following as purchasing questions, rather than treating each as a separate CBP mandate:

  • Who may enter the area where the goods will be stored, and who authorizes that access?
  • How are visitors and drivers kept within the areas relevant to their work?
  • Who can release inventory, and how are release instructions checked?
  • What happens when someone discovers unauthorized entry or evidence of tampering?

Ask for a walkthrough of the proposed procedures and a clear explanation of who owns each step. Keep the discussion specific to the goods and services you intend to purchase.

Check inspection and seal procedures before loading

According to CBP's criteria, procedures must verify container structural integrity before loading, including the reliability of door-locking mechanisms. CBP recommends a seven-point inspection covering the front wall, left side, right side, floor, ceiling or roof, inside and outside doors, and outside or undercarriage.

The criteria require a high-security seal on loaded CTPAT importer containers bound for the United States and specify that seals meet or exceed the current PAS ISO 17712 standards for high-security seals. Keep that shipment scope explicit when discussing requirements with a warehouse.

CBP also requires written procedures explaining how seals are controlled and attached. Procedures must address recognizing and reporting compromised seals or containers to CBP or the appropriate foreign authority. The document says only designated employees should distribute container seals.

Ask a prospective provider to explain who inspects the container, who issues and applies the seal, and how a suspected compromise is escalated. As a purchasing requirement, consider requesting a record connecting the inspection and seal details to the shipment.

Review responsibility when cargo changes hands

CBP's criteria require written, verifiable processes for screening and selecting business partners, including carriers, vendors and customers. They also call for periodic reviews of service-provider performance to identify security weaknesses. Documented screening should consider whether a provider can meet contractual security requirements and identify and correct deficiencies.

For applicable movements toward the U.S. border, CBP requires conveyance and trailer integrity to be maintained through a tracking and monitoring activity log or equivalent technology. Where driver logs are used, the criteria say those logs must reflect that trailer integrity was verified.

Turn those principles into a shipment responsibility map. Ask who has custody at receiving, storage, loading and dispatch; who records an exception; and who contacts your business. Request a sample exception workflow that shows how a damaged container, questionable seal or inventory discrepancy moves from discovery to a documented decision. These are useful purchasing requests, not a claim that every warehouse must use the same forms.

Connect the security review to your products

CBP recognizes that international supply chains differ and supports security measures based on risk analysis. Its criteria allow security plans to be customized to the participant's business model. For a buyer, that makes a product-specific discussion more useful than a general assurance that storage is secure.

For electronics and gaming inventory, ask how individual serial numbers would be recorded and reconciled during receiving, order preparation and returns. For luxury products, ask how receiving exceptions, packaging condition and release instructions would be documented. Treat these as proposed operating requirements for your account, rather than attributing them to CBP's container-security provisions.

Bring a sample product list and shipment workflow to the discussion. Ask the warehouse to identify which requested controls it can document in the service scope, who performs them and what information your team must supply. Evaluate the proposed process alongside any separately documented program credentials.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does every warehouse have to join CTPAT?

No. According to CBP, CTPAT participation is voluntary, and applicants must review the eligibility criteria for their business category. The third-party logistics criteria exclude entities providing only domestic services from that enrollment category.

What should buyers ask about 3PL services in Miami for high-value inventory?

Ask who can access the goods, authorize their release and resolve a discrepancy. Request an explanation of container inspections, seal handling and incident escalation where those activities apply. Separate your account's operating requirements from any claimed CTPAT status.

What do CBP's criteria say about compromised container seals?

CBP's criteria require procedures for recognizing and reporting compromised seals or containers to CBP or the appropriate foreign authority. They also require written procedures for controlling and attaching seals. Buyers should ask who handles the report and how the incident is documented.
