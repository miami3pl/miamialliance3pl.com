Ecommerce Air Hubs Lose Freighter Capacity: Lessons for Miami 3PL Users

Capacity cuts, not a demand surge, are pushing air cargo rates higher at peak, and that shapes how online sellers time and position inventory.

By Miami Alliance 3PL Team | November 27, 2026 | 8 min read

Freighter Capacity Into Europe Dropped Ahead of Black Friday

The Loadstar reported that air cargo rates are facing upward pressure, with capacity constraints, rather than a surge in peak season demand, increasingly setting prices. Europe offers the clearest example. Data published by aviation consultant Aevean shows freighter capacity from Asia and the Middle East into Europe fell 15% year on year in the two weeks to 24 November, equivalent to some 27,500 tonnes.

The decline was uneven. Maarten Wormer, head of consulting at Aevean, noted that "integrator and general cargo hubs are largely holding up, as ecommerce gateways take the hit". Among the eight major gateways tracked, Budapest recorded the steepest percentage decline at 36%, followed by Paris CDG at 27% and Liège at 24%, the latter losing some 5,800 tonnes of capacity.

Elsewhere, Frankfurt saw an 11% reduction, Cologne bucked the trend with a 16% increase, and capacity into Amsterdam stayed flat. For online sellers, the pattern is the point: the gateways most tied to ecommerce lost the most freighter space right before Black Friday.

Rates Are Climbing on Lost Capacity, Not Exceptional Demand

The capacity figures arrive as global airfreight rates climb again after a period of easing in the third quarter. According to Transport Intelligence's (Ti) latest Air Freight Rate Tracker, global headhaul rates reached $3.92 per kg in early November, up 7.6% on October, 8.7% against the Q3 average and 45.2% year on year. Backhaul rates climbed 6.2% month on month to $3 per kg, up 22.3% year on year.

Ti did not attribute those increases to exceptional demand growth. It pointed instead to the withdrawal of bellyhold capacity during winter passenger schedules, coupled with persistently high fuel costs. Because bellyhold space depends on passenger schedules, seasonal schedule changes move cargo capacity in and out of the market. Westbound transatlantic rates recorded the largest quarterly increase, at 21.7%, largely reflecting seasonal capacity reductions.

Demand still matters on some lanes. Technology shipments continue to underpin demand, and South Asia to North America remains the most expensive tradelane Ti tracks, at $7.08 per kg, up 70.5% year on year. The report added that strong demand for computing infrastructure, servers and semiconductors could see freighters redeployed to the Transpacific, further tightening capacity elsewhere.

Mixed Signals for the New Year and Ti's Contract Advice

There is little consensus over whether shippers can expect relief in the new year, The Loadstar reported. In Ti's industry survey, 72% of respondents anticipated rate increases over the next three months, and some 81% expected increases on the Asia-US West Coast trade. Ti itself expects prices to ease in the first quarter of next year, recalling that rates fell between Q4 and Q1 in each of the past four years.

Softer ecommerce, consumer and industrial demand after peak season should weigh on pricing, while sustained technology shipments and limited capacity are expected to cushion any decline. Ti also offered guidance for buyers of air freight:

  • Avoid locking in November rates on 12-month contracts.
  • Consider shorter-term agreements or index-linked pricing, which may offer greater flexibility.
  • On the transatlantic, consider delaying tenders until summer passenger schedules return in late March, when the additional bellyhold capacity could bring some relief.

Why Capacity, Not Just Demand, Should Shape Peak Planning

The core lesson is that a seller can face higher freight costs and tighter space even when order volume is not unusually strong. Ti described rates being set by how much space is available, and Aevean showed that space shrinking fastest at ecommerce gateways. For a brand that restocks by air during peak season, that can mean paying more for the same shipment, or waiting longer for space, when inventory matters most.

Several planning points follow:

  • Identify the products that depend on air. Items restocked by air are the most exposed when freighter space drops, so they deserve the first review.
  • Position inventory before the squeeze. Stock already sitting in a U.S. warehouse near customers does not have to compete for freighter space during the busiest weeks.
  • Keep freight terms flexible. Ti's caution against locking November rates into 12-month contracts is relevant to any seller negotiating air freight right now.
  • Budget for both outcomes after peak. Ti expects some easing, while most survey respondents expect further increases in the near term.
  • Ask about routing. Aevean's figures show capacity held up at integrator and general cargo hubs while ecommerce gateways lost space, so the airport a shipment moves through can make a difference.

What the Report Signals for U.S. and Latin American Sellers

Aevean's data covers Europe, but Ti's figures reach North America directly. South Asia to North America was the most expensive lane Ti tracked, and most survey respondents expected increases on the Asia-US West Coast trade. Importers bringing products from Asia into the United States by air are watching a market shaped by many of the same forces.

The report did not cover Latin American lanes. Still, the underlying logic travels: when passenger schedules shift or freighters are redeployed toward stronger lanes, space on other routes can tighten. Sellers who serve customers in both the United States and Latin America from one inventory pool may want to plan replenishment with that in mind, and for businesses comparing 3PL services in Miami, where inventory sits before peak is a lever worth reviewing.
