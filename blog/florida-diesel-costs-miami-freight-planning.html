Florida Diesel Costs Put Miami Freight Planning Under Pressure

Rising carrier expenses give Miami shippers a reason to review fuel charges, truck availability and warehouse handoffs together.

By Miami Alliance 3PL Team | October 30, 2026 | 6 min read

Looking for 3PL services in Miami? Get Your Quote →

Rising diesel costs are putting Florida trucking companies under financial pressure, FreightWaves reported, citing Florida Trucking Association CEO Scott Perry. Perry said fuel has overtaken driver compensation as the largest operating expense for some fleets, while insurance, equipment and wage costs also weigh on margins. For businesses moving inventory through Miami, the report gives a practical reason to review freight terms alongside warehouse receiving and distribution plans.

Key Takeaways

  • Perry told FreightWaves that higher fuel and other operating expenses are squeezing carrier margins.
  • According to Perry, improving freight rates partly reflect fewer available trucks rather than stronger demand.
  • Businesses should review how fuel charges apply before approving inbound and outbound transportation.
  • For Miami warehouse users, truck scheduling and inventory readiness belong in the same planning discussion.

Fuel pressure reaches beyond the diesel bill

FreightWaves reported that Florida carriers face a combination of rising diesel prices, insurance premiums, equipment costs and driver wages. Perry cited a roughly 70% year-over-year increase in fuel costs and said carriers without adequate fuel surcharge arrangements are particularly exposed. That figure should be understood as Perry's account of carrier cost pressure; it does not establish the increase for a particular shipper's transportation bill.

Perry also told FreightWaves he was unaware of association members that had closed or laid off workers specifically because of the recent fuel increases. His warning concerned mounting financial strain and the difficulty of sustaining operations when carriers cannot recover their expenses.

For a business owner, the useful next step is to examine the actual freight agreement. Ask how fuel charges are calculated, when they can change and which shipments they cover. Keep those answers with the transportation quote so purchasing and logistics staff can work from the same assumptions.

Treat truck availability as a separate planning issue

According to FreightWaves, Perry said Florida trucking companies were becoming somewhat more optimistic about freight rates heading into 2027. He cautioned that much of the improvement reflected trucking capacity leaving the market rather than a significant increase in freight demand. He also said rising diesel costs were absorbing much of the additional revenue.

For shippers, that distinction matters when interpreting a carrier's quote. A discussion about changing transportation costs should also include whether a truck is available for the intended pickup and delivery schedule. Avoid treating a favorable quote as confirmation that every operational detail has been settled.

Before committing inventory to a move, request confirmation of the pickup window, delivery arrangements and process for communicating changes. For businesses importing, exporting or distributing through Miami, include the warehouse handoff in that conversation. Frame this as preparation for the specific shipment, rather than assuming the report establishes a shortage on every route.

Review transportation and warehouse work together

The carrier pressures described by FreightWaves provide a useful starting point for a shipment review. Businesses using 3PL services in Miami should examine the work required before a truck arrives and after inventory reaches the warehouse. Start with the goods, their packaging, the intended destination and the tasks that must happen before release.

For an inbound shipment, confirm whether the receiving plan involves container unloading, pallet storage, inspection or preparation for onward distribution. For outbound goods, identify whether the order needs picking and packing, retailer compliance work or another preparation step. Ask the warehouse and transportation contacts to confirm the sequence before settling the schedule.

  • Document which transportation charges are included and which can change.
  • Confirm what information the warehouse needs before receiving the goods.
  • Identify who will communicate pickup or delivery changes.
  • Check that inventory preparation matches the planned truck arrival.

Use these questions to compare complete shipment plans. Any proposed change should be evaluated against the business's actual inventory needs and delivery commitments.

Equipment investment and parking also affect the discussion

FreightWaves reported that financial pressures are causing many carriers to delay equipment replacement, according to Perry. He warned that postponing investment could create further challenges involving reliability, productivity and driver retention. The article does not establish the condition of a particular carrier's fleet, so use this concern as a reason to ask operational questions.

Discuss how the carrier communicates an equipment problem and handles a disrupted pickup. Make sure the warehouse contact knows who should receive an update if the shipment timing changes. These conversations should focus on the arrangements for the load being booked.

Perry also described Florida's work to expand public-access truck parking, while emphasizing that the additional spaces would not eliminate the shortage. According to FreightWaves, he linked access to safe parking with driver safety and productivity. For shipment planning, ask carriers to propose workable schedules that account for driving and rest requirements rather than assuming they can absorb every timing change.

Match the warehouse service to the inventory plan

Decide whether goods need storage, preparation for individual orders, wholesale distribution or a transfer to onward transportation. Then ask for a service plan based on those requirements, with transportation assumptions clearly identified.
