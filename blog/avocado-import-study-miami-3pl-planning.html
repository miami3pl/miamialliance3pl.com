What the Avocado Study Measured

FreightWaves reported that Texas A&M University researchers estimated the U.S. economic contributions of Mexican Hass avocado imports. The transportation and warehousing portion amounted to $641.5 million and supported more than 4,150 jobs in fiscal year 2025-26. Across multiple industries, the analysis estimated $7.1 billion in total U.S. economic output and support for 37,163 jobs during the fiscal year ending June 2026.

Those figures describe different parts of the study. The transportation and warehousing estimate is narrower than the total economic output estimate. Neither should be presented as a forecast of sales for an individual warehouse or as a measure of demand for storage in Miami.

For an importer or distributor, the useful lesson is the range of activities involved after a purchase crosses a border. Consider reviewing your own logistics budget by task: transportation, receiving, storage, order preparation and distribution. Use your shipment plans to define those tasks, rather than applying the study's national totals to your business. The research provides context for that review, not a substitute for a shipment-specific operating plan.

Follow the Route Beyond the Border

According to FreightWaves, nearly 98% of Mexican avocados entering the U.S. in 2024 moved by truck through Texas land ports, primarily Pharr and Laredo. USDA data cited in the Texas A&M report assigned approximately 58.2% of those imports to Pharr and another 39.4% to Laredo. The story places Texas at the center of the border movement described in the study.

FreightWaves also described the network that follows clearance: trucking companies, refrigerated warehouses, wholesalers, distribution centers and food processors. The destination customers include grocery stores and restaurants. That sequence explains why the article's logistics implications extend beyond the crossing itself.

A business considering Miami should draw its own route before selecting warehouse services. Identify where the goods enter, where clearance takes place, which receiving location is proposed and where orders will go afterward. Do not assume that the avocado route described in the report includes Miami. Instead, compare the actual destinations and handling tasks in your plan with the services you are considering. Define Product Requirements Before Choosing Storage

The avocado story involves refrigerated transportation and distribution networks, according to FreightWaves. That detail belongs to the supply chain covered by the report. It should prompt a careful discussion of product requirements when evaluating a warehouse, rather than an assumption that every storage service suits the same goods.

Before requesting a proposal, prepare written handling instructions for the inventory you intend to store. Ask the prospective provider to assess the requested conditions and activities against the specific goods. Keep the conversation tied to the product and the work involved.

  • Describe the storage environment your product instructions call for.
  • Identify packaging conditions that staff should check at receiving.
  • Explain what should happen when a shipment arrives damaged or differs from the receiving instructions.
  • Specify any inspection or preparation requested before goods leave storage.

Treat those service descriptions as topics for a product-specific discussion. Ask the company to review the intended inventory and requested handling before deciding which service to use.

Plan the Handoff From Clearance to Receiving

FreightWaves describes avocado shipments moving into a wider distribution network once they clear the border. For businesses arranging their own imports, that transition is a useful point at which to review responsibilities. Build the receiving plan around the actual shipment, its clearance status and the instructions the receiving team will use.

Ask your logistics contacts to agree on who sends arrival updates, who confirms that goods are ready for delivery and who responds to discrepancies. Prepare a receiving reference that identifies the expected goods and explains how to handle differences between the shipment and the instructions. These are planning recommendations, not customs requirements drawn from the study.

Miami Alliance 3PL coordinates receiving of goods after customs clearance with the client's licensed customs broker. A buyer can use that service description to structure the handoff: discuss the proposed arrival, the information needed for receiving and the next action after the goods are accepted. If your distribution plan calls for storage, cross-docking or order preparation, identify that intended next step during the receiving discussion.

Specify How Inventory Will Leave the Warehouse

According to FreightWaves, the avocado distribution network serves several kinds of customers, including grocery stores, restaurants and food processors. For your own inventory, use that variety as a prompt to describe the destination and order format before choosing services. Start with the orders you expect the warehouse to prepare.

For wholesale work, provide the intended retailer instructions and discuss the requested preparation. For ecommerce, explain how orders should be picked, packed and shipped. If the plan includes bundles or returns, include those tasks in the scope you ask the provider to review. Avoid leaving them implicit in a general request for warehouse space.
