DDP Customs Risks Put Miami Import Receiving Plans in Focus

A CargoTrans warning puts supplier transparency, customs records and warehouse receiving plans under scrutiny for businesses importing through Miami.

By Miami Alliance 3PL Team | November 5, 2026 | 7 min read

Looking for 3PL services in Miami? Get Your Quote →

The Loadstar reported that CargoTrans is warning U.S. buyers about compliance and financial exposure under delivered duty-paid shipping arrangements as Customs and Border Protection increases scrutiny of foreign importers of record. The warning includes potential penalties, shipment seizures and refused entries, even when the buyer is not named as the importer of record. For businesses moving inventory into Miami, it is a reason to review customs documentation before building receiving and fulfillment plans around an expected arrival.

Key Takeaways

  • According to The Loadstar, CargoTrans warned that DDP arrangements can leave U.S. buyers exposed even when an overseas seller handles import formalities.

  • Miami businesses should connect customs status with warehouse receiving plans and review shipment roles with their licensed customs broker.

Why the DDP Warning Matters to U.S. Buyers

According to The Loadstar, sellers assume responsibility for import clearance and associated duties under delivered duty-paid, or DDP, arrangements. CargoTrans executives warned that this allocation of responsibility does not necessarily shield the U.S. buyer from customs exposure. Their concern was particularly focused on arrangements involving foreign sellers or intermediary platforms acting as non-resident importers of record.

The Loadstar reported that CargoTrans sees increasing scrutiny of whether these arrangements are being used to avoid tariffs. The executives described potential consequences including refused entry, seizure and re-export. They also warned that penalties could extend to U.S. buyers and their import bonds.

For a business owner, the practical review should begin with visibility. Ask who is handling the import formalities, what information is being declared and whether your business can obtain the underlying records. Treat a supplier's agreement to manage clearance as a reason to document responsibilities clearly.

For businesses buying 3PL services in Miami, this review should sit alongside inventory planning. Before scheduling receiving or preparing customer orders around an incoming shipment, establish how your team will confirm customs status and communicate changes to the warehouse.

Check the Importer and Consignee Information

The Loadstar reported that CargoTrans had heard from U.S. buyers whose shipments were refused entry, seized or ordered for re-export. The examples included incorrect importer information and the use of a third-party logistics provider as the ultimate consignee. Those reported cases make the shipment's named parties a useful starting point for a documentation review.

The report does not establish that every shipment listing a logistics provider has a problem. It does give buyers a reason to examine how their own arrangements are documented. Ask your licensed customs broker to review the importer and consignee information against the transaction documents and the intended receiving arrangement.

  • Identify the parties shown on the customs paperwork and compare them with the supplier's transaction documents.
  • Ask the supplier to explain information your team cannot reconcile.
  • Review any proposed use of the warehouse's name or address with the warehouse and your licensed customs broker.
  • Keep responsibility for resolving documentation questions explicit before arranging delivery.

This is also a useful handoff point between purchasing and logistics. Give the receiving team a clear contact for customs status, and make sure the person managing the supplier knows which paperwork questions remain open.

Request Customs Records Before a Shipment Problem

For businesses continuing to use DDP, CargoTrans recommended greater supplier transparency, according to The Loadstar. That included access to customs declarations, transaction documents and supporting import data. CargoTrans also warned that buyers could not simply rely on being unaware of what had been declared on their behalf.

Turn that recommendation into a supplier discussion before the next shipment. Ask which records will be available, who will provide them and how your team should raise discrepancies. If the supplier's explanation and the documents do not line up, bring the issue to your licensed customs broker for review.

Keep the internal review connected to the commercial transaction. Purchasing can compare the documents with what was ordered, finance can examine the transaction records, and logistics can check the planned movement and receiving details. Refer unresolved customs questions to the people responsible for compliance.

For warehouse planning, use the review to identify open issues that could affect the expected handoff. Consider keeping inventory with unresolved customs status out of firm receiving commitments. Discuss how you would adjust fulfillment or distribution plans if entry were refused or the goods were ordered for re-export, both outcomes described in The Loadstar's report.

Coordinate Internally Before Answering Customs

The Loadstar reported that CargoTrans raised concerns about growing numbers of CBP requests for information, known as CF28s. CargoTrans urged importers to investigate their records thoroughly before responding and cautioned against rushing toward the standard 20-day response deadline without checking information across procurement, finance, legal, compliance and logistics.

The concern, as reported by The Loadstar, is that a discrepancy between supplier documentation and the original declaration could reveal a previously unreported compliance problem. CargoTrans also described a shift toward notices of action issued without an earlier request for additional information, attributing this to greater use of customs data analytics.

For an importer, the operational takeaway is to establish a review process before a request arrives. Decide who collects records, who compares the documents and who coordinates the response. Keep the applicable deadline visible while giving the responsible teams an opportunity to examine inconsistencies.

Include warehouse-facing logistics staff in that communication when the matter affects receiving plans. Ask them to distinguish an expected arrival from a confirmed delivery arrangement. That gives purchasing, fulfillment and distribution teams a common basis for deciding whether to revise their plans while customs questions remain unresolved.

Review Shipping Terms Alongside Total Movement Costs

According to The Loadstar, CargoTrans suggested that some businesses reconsider DDP and potentially switch to delivered at place, or DAP, to retain control of import formalities. The report presents this as an option to evaluate. A buyer should review the proposed responsibilities with its supplier and licensed customs broker before changing terms.

Use that discussion to compare visibility as well as the quoted transportation cost. Ask who will control the import paperwork, how your business will obtain records and who will communicate clearance status to the team arranging warehouse delivery. Consider whether your staff is prepared to manage the responsibilities of the proposed arrangement.

The Loadstar also reported CargoTrans's central warning that cheaper ocean freight offers little benefit when customs exposure and inland disruption increase the overall cost of moving goods. For a Miami importer, that is a useful framework for reviewing a shipment from supplier handoff through warehouse receiving.

Connect the review to the work planned after arrival. Identify which goods are intended for ecommerce fulfillment, which are allocated to wholesale distribution and which are planned for Latin America distribution from Miami. Then decide which commitments need revision if the receiving date changes.
