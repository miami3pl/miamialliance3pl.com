What Happened at Brazil's Biggest Air Cargo Gateway

The Loadstar reported that São Paulo's Guarulhos Airport, Brazil's busiest international air cargo hub, is struggling to move freight after tighter export inspections filled its warehouse. The effect has been a jam at the gate: some trucks have waited days to unload, and imports that had already cleared customs have been slow to be handed over.

Kuehne+Nagel said on Tuesday that some trucks which arrived at the airport on Friday, November 20, were still waiting to unload. Some airlines had been unable to collect freight from the terminal, which left less room for new shipments. When cargo cannot leave a warehouse, new cargo cannot come in, and the line of trucks outside grows.

Brazilian forwarder VL Cargo said on Monday that acceptance of new export freight had been temporarily suspended and advised customers to hold deliveries. The airport said the same day that its terminal remained open and asked hauliers to confirm a slot before sending trucks. According to The Loadstar, the airport said perishables, live animals and special cargo were being handled as usual, and it gave no recovery date.

Lufthansa Cargo told customers it would rebook outstanding shipments, Brazilian trade outlet FUP News reported. The carrier said the terminal had temporarily stopped handing general cargo over to airlines and halted acceptance of new freight into storage when the warehouse reached capacity.

Why the Backlog Built Up

Accounts of the cause differ in their detail. Kuehne+Nagel said the disruption followed a Federal Police operation from November 18 to 22 and that police and customs officers were continuing inspections at cargo acceptance points and in airline storage areas. Brazil's Federal Police announced an anti-trafficking operation at Guarulhos on November 18. The airport, for its part, attributed the backlog to "intensified checks on dry export cargo," without identifying a specific operation, The Loadstar reported.

The export problem also landed on top of an older one. According to The Loadstar, the airport warned in June that imports were arriving faster than cleared freight was being collected, tying up warehouse space and slowing receiving, storage and handover. It flagged the same imbalance again on November 12. The terminal had already reported strain on the import side before the export inspections tightened.

The lesson for shippers is structural rather than specific to Brazil. A cargo terminal works like a shared warehouse. When outbound freight is held for inspection and inbound freight is not picked up, both sides compete for the same floor space. Customs clearance by itself does not put goods in a buyer's hands; the freight still has to be released, collected and moved.

What Moves Through Guarulhos

The scale of the hub explains why a backlog there matters. Guarulhos accounted for 59% of Brazil's air exports and 55% of its air imports in 2025, according to the airport operator. The cargo terminal reported a record 99,500 tonnes in the third quarter of 2026, up 14.4% year on year, excluding courier shipments. The Loadstar reported this breakdown of that freight:

  • Automotive freight was the largest segment at 20%.
  • Pharmaceuticals, chemicals and hospital supplies followed at 19%.
  • Machinery made up 12%.

On the export side, fruit and eggs accounted for 36% of Guarulhos's air export tonnage in 2024, with papayas and mangoes making up nearly four-fifths of fruit shipments. Electrical and electronic goods and chemical industry products, including medicines, were the two largest import categories by weight, according to São Paulo state's logistics plan.

That mix touches many kinds of businesses that trade with Brazil: parts suppliers, distributors of medical and hospital supplies, electronics sellers and food exporters. Any of them could see orders slip when freight sits in a terminal instead of moving.

Planning Lessons for Importers and Exporters

The Guarulhos backlog has no announced end date, so the useful response is planning rather than prediction. A few practical points apply to any business whose goods move by air through a single large gateway:

  • Treat cleared freight as not yet available. The Loadstar's report shows imports that had cleared customs still waiting for handover. Lead times should include the time needed to collect and move goods after clearance.
  • Follow slot and acceptance instructions. The airport asked hauliers to confirm a slot before sending trucks, and VL Cargo told customers to hold deliveries. Sending freight to a closed acceptance point adds cost and confusion.
  • Expect rebooking. Lufthansa Cargo told customers it would rebook outstanding shipments. Rebookings can push arrival dates back, which matters for promotions and retailer delivery windows.
  • Hold a buffer where it counts. Inventory already sitting in a U.S. warehouse is not exposed to a terminal backlog abroad. For fast-moving items, extra stock held closer to customers can absorb a delay.
  • Keep documents complete. Tighter inspections give unclear paperwork more chances to cause questions. Working with a licensed customs broker on accurate commercial documents is part of planning for inspection-heavy periods.

The receiving end deserves the same attention. When held freight finally moves, it often arrives in a bunch, and the warehouse that receives it needs space and labor ready. Signals to Watch Next

Because the airport gave no recovery date, shippers will rely on updates from the parties closest to the freight. Useful signals include:

  • Statements from the airport operator on acceptance of new export freight and on handover of cleared imports.
  • Notices from forwarders such as VL Cargo and Kuehne+Nagel on whether customers should resume deliveries.
  • Carrier notices, such as Lufthansa Cargo's rebooking message, on when general cargo is moving to airlines again.
  • Updates on the inspections that police and customs officers were carrying out at cargo acceptance points and in airline storage areas.

Plans built on a fixed recovery date carry risk while no date has been given. A more cautious approach is to keep orders flowing from stock already in the United States and to schedule new shipments once acceptance and handover are confirmed.
