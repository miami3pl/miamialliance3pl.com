De Minimis Suspension Puts Miami Import Data Under Scrutiny

Consistent product records and clear responsibility for shipment information can help importers prepare for customs clearance and warehouse receiving.

By Miami Alliance 3PL Team | October 15, 2026 | 7 min read

Supply Chain Brain reported that the suspension of duty-free de minimis treatment has made shipment data a broader operational concern for importers. Formerly eligible shipments now need the applicable customs entry process and supporting information. For businesses moving goods into Miami, that makes the consistency of supplier records, broker instructions and shipment documents an issue for inventory planning as well as customs filing.

Low-Value Goods Still Need Supportable Entry Information

According to Supply Chain Brain, eligible goods valued at $800 or less previously could enter the United States without duty or tax through simplified clearance procedures. With that treatment suspended, formerly eligible shipments must follow the applicable customs entry process. The publication distinguishes non-postal shipments, which need an appropriate formal or informal entry procedure, from certain mail shipments that may use a separate postal process.

The operational concern extends beyond paying duties. Supply Chain Brain reported that required information may include a 10-digit Harmonized Tariff Schedule classification, customs value, country of origin and applicable partner government agency requirements, depending on the goods and entry type. Having goods packed for collection does not establish that the filing information is complete.

For a business planning a Miami warehouse arrival, the practical recommendation is to review shipment readiness alongside document readiness. Ask the customs broker to identify the information needed for the applicable entry, then assign responsibility for supplying it. A planned collection or delivery should prompt that review rather than serve as evidence that customs preparation is finished.

A Sales Description Is Only Part of the Product Record

Supply Chain Brain describes defensible data as information that is consistent and supportable across every party, system and document involved in a shipment. That standard is different from having a product listing suitable for an online customer. The publication explains that a general clothing description may support a storefront listing while lacking the detail needed to determine classification, duty treatment or admissibility.

Importers should therefore review the underlying product record before reusing sales information for customs instructions. Identify which details come from the manufacturer, which come from purchasing records and which need specialist review. Where a description is incomplete, request the missing specifications instead of assuming that a familiar product name will answer the broker's questions.

The same review should cover the meaning of quantities and values. Supply Chain Brain notes that one party may interpret a quantity as individual items while another reads it as cartons or pallets. It also identifies confusion between country of origin and country of export, and discrepancies between invoice value and customs value. Treat these as separate fields to reconcile, with supporting information available for review.

Assign Responsibility Before Documents Reach the Broker

According to Supply Chain Brain, shipment information often sits with different participants. Manufacturers hold technical specifications, importers maintain purchasing and product records, and transport providers work from shipping documents. The customs broker may receive filing instructions through yet another system. That distribution of information makes agreement about ownership and timing essential.

A useful preparation step is to map each required field to its source and the person responsible for resolving questions. Keep the process specific enough that an incomplete origin statement or an unclear quantity has an assigned next action. Importers should also agree with their suppliers and broker on when the information must be ready for review.

  • Ask the broker which fields and supporting details the entry requires.
  • Identify who supplies each field and who reviews discrepancies.
  • Agree whether quantities represent items, cartons or pallets.
  • Reconcile descriptions, origin information and values across the relevant records.
  • Record unresolved issues before using the shipment to support downstream inventory plans.

Supply Chain Brain emphasizes that using a customs broker does not remove the importer of record's responsibility for entry accuracy. Delegating document preparation should therefore include a clear process for checking and correcting the information.

Use Technology to Expose Gaps, With Human Review

Supply Chain Brain describes several ways technology can support the customs workflow. Document extraction can reduce manual entry, classification tools can recommend codes for review, and screening tools can flag restricted parties or controlled goods before booking. Filing systems can reuse approved shipment data, check required fields and transmit filings electronically.

For an importer assessing those capabilities, start with the recurring problem. If staff repeatedly copy the same information between documents, consider where approved data could be reused. If descriptions arrive incomplete, define the missing information before expecting software to resolve the issue. A field that contains text still deserves review when its meaning is unclear or inconsistent with another record.

The publication also cautions that technology does not replace human judgment. Classification, valuation and origin may require specialist review. Treat automated recommendations as inputs to that review, and make responsibility for the final information explicit. Supply Chain Brain notes that access to customs responses and entry status can show which shipments need attention; use that visibility to guide follow-up on exceptions rather than assuming that electronic submission has resolved every issue.

Correct the Source of Repeated Filing Problems

Resolving a broker query can address the immediate shipment without changing what caused the problem. Supply Chain Brain recommends monitoring broker questions, filing errors and customs responses for recurring patterns. A repeatedly challenged description may point to a product record or invoice template that needs correction. Repeatedly incomplete origin information may call for a change in the supplier's process.

Make that review part of shipment follow-up. Record what was questioned, where the information originated and what correction was accepted. Then decide whether the underlying record, template or supplier instruction needs revision. Before the next shipment uses the same information, check that the correction has reached the people preparing the documents.

For businesses relying on incoming stock to support sales or distribution, this is also a planning exercise. Supply Chain Brain connects more predictable clearance with inventory planning, delivery commitments and margin management. The practical response is to keep unresolved customs information visible when reviewing expected inventory. Accurate records can reduce the risk of preventable holds and corrections, according to the publication, but a completed internal checklist should not be treated as a promise of clearance.
