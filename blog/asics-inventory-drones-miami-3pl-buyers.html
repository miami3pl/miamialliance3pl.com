What ASICS Changed in Its Inventory Checks

According to FreightWaves, ASICS introduced autonomous drones manufactured by Corvus Robotics to inspect storage areas where inventory discrepancies were most common. Those areas included case reserve and the singles area, where returned products and individual pairs are stored and picked. Both areas had previously been counted twice a year under the company's cycle count policy.

FreightWaves reported that the drones increased counting frequency six-fold. Flights take place nightly, with the collected data aggregated and analyzed monthly. The distinction matters when reviewing the story: flight frequency, analysis frequency and the time needed to resolve a discrepancy describe different parts of the process.

For a business evaluating warehouse support, use that distinction to structure the conversation. Ask how often stock is checked, when findings reach the inventory team and who handles a mismatch. Request a walkthrough from discovery to correction. A useful review should explain both how the warehouse detects a problem and how someone determines what needs to change in the inventory record.

Misplaced Stock Can Affect What Customers Can Buy

FreightWaves reported that, during a three-month stretch, the drones found 26 misplaced cases containing more than 200 pairs of shoes. The article said those products otherwise would have remained unavailable to customers until the next scheduled physical count. An ASICS distribution executive described how a missing unit could lead to cancellation of an order already accepted.

The report also connected inventory confidence with marketplace availability. According to the announcement described by FreightWaves, ASICS previously held back 12 to 15 units out of every 100 from its availability feeds to avoid promising stock it could not deliver. ASICS said it now publishes the full feed and sells that product at full price.

Use those results as prompts for reviewing your own inventory decisions. Ask which stock is physically present, which stock is available for orders and which stock is being withheld while a discrepancy is investigated. If your business reserves inventory as a precaution, document the reason and review what evidence would support changing that practice. The ASICS results do not establish an appropriate reserve level for another business.

Returns Show Why Product Scans Need Context

The most useful operational detail in the FreightWaves report concerns how the drones check their work. On the same pass, they read Universal Product Codes and count boxes at each location. In the singles area, returned goods mean the same stock keeping unit can appear several times in a location. The article explained that a product-code read alone can become associated with the wrong box.

According to Corvus, as reported by FreightWaves, a count of 10 boxes should produce 10 product-code reads. The physical total provides a check against the scan results. This example shows why the ASICS process considers both product identity and quantity when examining a storage location.

For your returns workflow, ask the warehouse provider to explain how it confirms the item, its quantity and its recorded location. Agree on how questionable records should be investigated before you make those units available for sale. When discussing Miami 3PL services, bring a representative returns scenario and ask the team to walk through the handling steps relevant to your inventory.

The Report Still Gives Inventory Staff a Central Role

FreightWaves reported that an inventory associate reviews each flight every morning and resolves most discrepancies from a desk using the captured video and images. The companies said the system saved about 200 hours of monthly counting labor. ASICS redirected that time into inventory research and exception management, including reconciliation in the packing operation, root cause research and wholesale projects.

That staffing detail is relevant when assessing a proposed inventory process. Ask who reviews the evidence, who authorizes a correction and how unresolved cases are followed up. Include the packing operation in the discussion, since the ASICS example specifically identifies it as an area receiving additional reconciliation work.

Ask for an example of how a recurring discrepancy would be investigated. Your review might follow a returned item, a misplaced case or a mismatch discovered during packing. Specify what information your business needs when an issue remains open, such as the affected item, the recorded location and the next review step. Treat these as requirements to discuss with a provider, rather than capabilities to assume from a technology description.

Review Inventory Across Your Sales Channels

According to FreightWaves, the ASICS operation fills ecommerce orders and serves wholesale accounts as well as the company's full-price and outlet stores. Its inventory is managed in a Manhattan Associates warehouse management system. The report therefore offers an example of inventory checking within an operation serving several types of orders.

For your own warehouse review, map the channels that will draw on stored inventory. Discuss how an unresolved discrepancy should affect ecommerce availability, wholesale commitments and planned distribution. Ask which records your team should use when deciding whether to accept an order or investigate a shortage. Keep the discussion tied to the actual order types your business handles.
