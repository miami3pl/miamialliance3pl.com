FreightWaves: The Truck Count Is at Its Lowest Since Early 2022

FreightWaves reported that the U.S. trucking market shed roughly 100,000 for-hire tractors over the past year, including about 51,000 in a single month this past summer. According to FreightWaves data, the losses pushed the active tractor count to its lowest level since early 2022. The publication called the capacity exodus "the clearest signal yet that the prolonged freight recession has permanently squeezed out a meaningful share of the carrier base, particularly among small fleets."

The shrinkage is uneven. FreightWaves reported that fleets running between 20 and 1,000 trucks are up 12% over the last five years, while the edges of the industry are contracting:

  • The smallest carriers: operators with 1 to 6 trucks have erased their entire pandemic-era expansion.
  • The largest fleets: carriers with more than 1,000 trucks are down about 9%, as larger carriers deliberately park equipment to maximize utilization rather than grow headcount.

The middle of the industry has grown while the smallest and largest operators have pulled back. For shippers, that shift affects who is available to haul freight and on what terms, even in a market the report describes as recovering.

Why Small Carriers Are Struggling as Rates Rise

Rising rates have not stopped the exits. In the interview FreightWaves summarized, Van de Kamp said the root cause is a lag effect: rates are rising but have not climbed fast enough to rescue carriers whose finances were battered during a prolonged period when rates ran well below costs.

According to the report, other pressures are stacking on top of that lag:

  • Fuel: diesel topping $6 a gallon adds pressure for any operator not fully offsetting fuel costs through surcharges.
  • Regulation and liability: increased regulation and liability concerns are compounding the squeeze on small fleets.
  • Drivers: large carriers face a persistent driver recruiting and retention challenge.

For carriers, Van de Kamp offered a pointed reminder: "Really understand your true costs when pricing out that freight." The advice is aimed at trucking companies, but it fits the businesses that buy freight and logistics services too. A shipper that knows its full cost per order, from port pickup to the customer's door, is in a better position to judge whether a rate increase, a mode change or a different stock location makes sense.

Spot Rates, Contract Rates and Tender Rejections by the Numbers

On the rate side, FreightWaves reported that conditions are improving for carriers, which for shippers points to higher freight costs. The report put the all-in spot rate (NTI USA) at $360 as of Friday, November 13, up 2.86% month over month. Other figures it cited:

  • Truckload contract rates (VCRPM1 linehaul): $2.88, a 25% jump year over year.
  • Intermodal contract rates (IMCRPM1 USA): $1.78, up more than 6.5% year over year. FreightWaves said that level still represents substantial savings versus truck for shippers willing to convert freight.
  • Overall tender rejections (STRI): 12.87%, just below 13% and down roughly 1% month over month, but supported by the tightening tractor supply.
  • Differences by equipment: flatbed tender rejections are hovering around 20% to 21%, a level that has held since late September, while dry van rejections are softer at 10.4%.
  • Volumes (STVI): up about 4% from October 1 (11,068) to 11,405, even as the index slipped 3% to 4% week over week.

Van de Kamp also flagged early signs of pre-peak tightening out of California and rising volumes in parts of Texas and the western Midwest, including Kansas. For a business shipping out of South Florida, the point is not any single number. Truck capacity is thinner, contract pricing is well above where it was a year ago, and conditions differ by region and equipment type.

What the Advice to Shippers Looks Like in Practice

Van de Kamp advised shippers to budget for higher contract rates into 2027, pursue mode conversion to intermodal where feasible, and monitor demand trends. Each point leads to decisions a business can review now.

Budget with current contract rates in mind

With truckload contract rates up 25% year over year in the FreightWaves data, a freight budget built on last year's prices may already be out of date. A useful starting point is to list which shipments depend on long truck hauls and which could travel a shorter distance from a better-placed stock location.

Review mode and shipment size

Not every product or lane fits intermodal, and time-sensitive orders may not. Planned replenishment moves are usually easier to shift. Consolidating inbound freight into fewer, fuller loads and breaking it down at a warehouse is another way to limit how many truck moves a business has to buy.

Cut unnecessary handoffs

Each time goods move between a port, a yard, a warehouse and a final carrier, a business pays for another move and depends on another truck being available. Receiving containers at one location close to the port and shipping orders directly from that stock can remove some of those steps.
