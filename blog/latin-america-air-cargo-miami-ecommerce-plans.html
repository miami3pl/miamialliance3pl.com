Latin America Air Cargo Shifts Test Miami Ecommerce Plans

Growing online retail shipments are changing cargo networks, making route-specific planning a priority for businesses distributing through Miami.

Ecommerce imports are growing across Latin America, but they have not resolved the region's air cargo imbalance, The Loadstar reported. Airline executives described declining industrial shipments, changing routes and competing demands for aircraft. For businesses distributing through Miami, the report supports reviewing specific shipping lanes and warehouse receiving plans instead of treating ecommerce growth as evidence of available capacity.

Ecommerce Growth Leaves an Uneven Cargo Picture

According to The Loadstar, LATAM Cargo group CEO Andrés Bianchi said stronger ecommerce volumes were being partly offset by declines in other commodities. Auto parts and mining equipment were among the industrial goods affected. Latin America's export sector remained strong, leaving the directional imbalance unresolved despite the growth in online retail shipments.

That distinction matters when interpreting the headline for a business shipping through Miami. Growth in a broad category does not establish what an airline can accommodate on a particular route. A useful planning response is to ask about the intended shipment, destination and departure rather than using regional ecommerce demand as a substitute for those details.

For businesses evaluating 3PL services in Miami, start with a receiving plan that identifies which inbound goods support which outbound commitments. Separate confirmed shipment information from tentative arrival expectations. Ask the warehouse what information it needs when an arrival changes, and decide who will communicate that change. The practical focus should be the shipment's status and handling instructions, with regional market news providing context for those conversations.

Miami Connects a Network of Different Destinations

The Loadstar reported that ecommerce had become a major driver of Avianca Cargo's southbound business, particularly between Miami and Colombia. The carrier also moved ecommerce into Brazil, Chile, Ecuador and Central America. Its resumed freighter operations from Miami to Venezuela carried online retail shipments as well.

Avianca Cargo CEO Diogo Elias described a network with concentrated inbound origins and a much broader spread of outbound destinations. According to The Loadstar, around 90% of the carrier's South American cargo into Miami came from five principal origins, while its Miami network served more than 30 destinations. The balancing task therefore extended beyond matching the outbound and return movements of an individual route.

For an importer or distributor, the practical takeaway is to plan by destination. Keep inventory intended for different markets identifiable in receiving and fulfillment instructions. Before committing goods to an outbound movement, review the destination, allocation and shipment status together. Businesses considering Latin America distribution from Miami should use those details to discuss their warehouse requirements, rather than assuming that every market connected through Miami presents the same operating picture.

Aircraft Redeployment Complicates Capacity Assumptions

The Loadstar also described how changing ecommerce demand affected aircraft deployment. Atlas Air executive Martin Drew said online platforms redirected shipments toward alternative markets following the removal of U.S. de minimis treatment for Chinese shipments. He cited Lima as a market where a platform requested capacity and the airline continued operating flights from China.

According to Drew's account in The Loadstar, that shift created a further challenge when U.S. demand recovered. Aircraft already committed to Latin America were no longer readily available to accommodate the additional U.S. business. The example illustrates the difference between demand emerging in a market and an airline having aircraft available to serve it.

For warehouse planning, treat a proposed routing change as a reason to revisit the receiving schedule. Ask whether the expected arrival remains confirmed and whether outbound orders depend on that incoming inventory. Consider which customer commitments need review if the shipment moves. Keep transport decisions and warehouse instructions connected: a revised flight plan should prompt a check of the receiving information, inventory allocation and planned release of goods.

Alternative Routes Put the Actual Shipment Path First

Not all Chinese ecommerce destined for Latin America passes through the United States, The Loadstar reported. Elias identified Madrid as Avianca's principal European gateway for that traffic, with its passenger network connecting Europe and South America. The report also described indirect capacity and transhipments serving demand between Asia and Latin America.

In a conference presentation covered by The Loadstar, Rotate's Ryan Keyrouse warned that an apparent global balance between supply and demand would not necessarily apply to individual trade lanes. For a business owner, that is a reason to distinguish the overall market picture from the actual path assigned to a shipment.

Build the warehouse discussion around goods expected to pass through Miami. Identify their origin, intended destination and receiving status, and flag any proposed routing changes before finalizing instructions. If the shipment path changes, review whether the original Miami receiving and distribution plan still applies. Avoid treating a route described in industry coverage as an available option for a particular order; ask the transport provider to confirm the proposed movement before aligning warehouse activity around it.

Match Warehouse Instructions to the Cargo Mix

The Loadstar reported that Bianchi viewed ecommerce as more exposed to regulatory cycles than other commodities. He also pointed to the growing importance of pharmaceuticals while noting their distinct vulnerabilities: flows could decline when patents expired and local generic production developed. LATAM's stated approach was to retain the ability to adapt rather than bet on a single growth category.

For a business using a Miami warehouse, a practical application is to review the product mix alongside shipment volume. When the assortment changes, revisit product descriptions, handling needs, storage requirements and outbound preparation instructions. Do not assume instructions written for one category adequately describe another. Ask the logistics team to review the proposed inventory before arranging receiving.
