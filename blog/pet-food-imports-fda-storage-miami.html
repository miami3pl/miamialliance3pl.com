Start with the product, ingredients, and label

According to FDA, the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act requires animal food, including pet food, to be safe to eat, produced under sanitary conditions, contain no harmful substances, and be truthfully labeled. FDA also says canned pet food must meet the applicable low-acid canned food processing regulations. Import planning should therefore begin with the actual product and how it is made.

FDA explains that ingredients must be safe and serve an appropriate function. Some ingredients do not require pre-market approval, while other substances must qualify as generally recognized as safe for their intended use or have food-additive approval. Colorings require approval for the intended use. Ask the supplier to explain the basis for the ingredients used in your product.

FDA identifies these federal labeling elements:

  • Proper identification of the product.
  • A net quantity statement.
  • The name and place of business of the manufacturer or distributor.
  • Ingredients listed from most to least by weight.

FDA notes that some states enforce their own labeling regulations and that the agency reviews certain pet food claims. Before preparing inventory for fulfillment, review the intended label and claims, and identify any state labeling requirements that need further attention.

Identify who holds the FSVP responsibility

FDA defines the importer for Foreign Supplier Verification Program purposes as the U.S. owner or consignee of food offered for import. If neither exists, FDA identifies the U.S. agent or representative of the foreign owner or consignee at entry, with a signed statement of consent. Establish which business fills that role before organizing the shipment.

According to FDA, covered importers must verify that foreign suppliers produce food with the applicable level of public health protection and that the food is not adulterated. The importer must develop, maintain, and follow an FSVP for each food and its foreign supplier. Buying the same food from different suppliers requires separate programs for those suppliers; buying different foods from one supplier also requires separate programs.

For planning, build a food-and-supplier list and identify who maintains the associated records. FDA describes modified requirements for certain very small importers and certain small suppliers, plus circumstances affecting importers that also manufacture or process food. Check which provisions apply before deciding what documentation is sufficient. Treat the storage agreement and the assignment of importer responsibilities as separate planning decisions.

Connect supplier verification to the actual food risks

FDA says covered importers must identify and evaluate known or reasonably foreseeable hazards for each type of food. The analysis considers the likelihood of a hazard without controls and the severity of resulting illness or injury. FDA identifies biological and chemical hazards and directs importers to consider factors such as formulation, equipment, and manufacturing and packing procedures.

The supplier evaluation also considers who controls the hazards, the supplier's food safety practices, compliance information, and its history of correcting problems. FDA includes storage and transportation practices among the relevant factors. For your planning file, request product-specific handling information and consider it alongside the supplier evaluation.

According to FDA, verification activities depend on food risks and supplier characteristics. Options described in its reference include reviewing relevant food safety records and conducting on-site audits. Annual on-site audits are generally required for certain serious hazards controlled by the supplier, although FDA allows an appropriately documented alternative that provides adequate assurances.

FDA says importers must establish and follow written procedures for using approved suppliers, with a limited temporary route for unapproved suppliers subject to adequate verification before import. Prepare the supplier approval decision and supporting records before scheduling receiving.

Keep the program current and define corrective action

According to FDA, the evaluation of food risk and supplier performance must be revisited at least every three years, or when new information emerges about a potential hazard or supplier performance. A supplier file should therefore support ongoing review as well as the initial purchasing decision.

FDA also requires prompt, appropriate corrective action when an importer determines that a supplier's processes or food fail the applicable requirements. Depending on the circumstances, this can include discontinuing use of the supplier until the cause has been adequately addressed.

As an operating step, establish who receives supplier problem reports, who decides what action to take, and who communicates inventory instructions to the warehouse. Document how changes to a product or its handling requirements will reach the people arranging fulfillment. These are preparation steps for coordinating the business; they are not additional FDA requirements stated in the supplied references.

FDA provides a list of required FSVP records and an Importer Portal for submitting them. Use the agency's recordkeeping resources to check your documentation rather than assuming that purchasing or warehouse records alone cover the program.

Turn the product review into a receiving plan

FDA says its risk-based inspections examine whether animal food is manufactured, processed, packed, and held in a way that prevents contamination or adulteration. For a business arranging storage, that makes the product's handling needs a practical part of warehouse selection.

Prepare a written brief for the prospective logistics provider. As planning recommendations, include:

  • The product description, packaging format, and supplier's storage and handling instructions.
  • Any temperature or other environmental conditions the product requires.
  • The product and shipment identifiers you want used when communicating inventory instructions.
  • The steps you want followed when packaging arrives damaged or product condition is uncertain.
  • The contact authorized to resolve receiving issues and give fulfillment instructions.

Review that brief before agreeing on the storage arrangement. For online orders and wholesale distribution, also describe how goods should be picked, packed, and presented for shipment. Keep product requirements consistent across supplier documents and warehouse instructions so the receiving discussion starts with a clear description of the inventory.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does FDA regulate imported pet food?

FDA regulates pet food as animal food and says it must be safe, produced under sanitary conditions, free of harmful substances, and truthfully labeled. Its FSVP rule also establishes foreign supplier verification responsibilities for covered importers of animal food.

Can one FSVP cover every food purchased from a supplier?

According to FDA, covered importers must maintain an FSVP for each food and its foreign supplier. Different foods from the same supplier require separate programs, as does the same food purchased from different suppliers.
