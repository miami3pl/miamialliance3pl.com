Understand what FDA guidance does

FDA's Fish and Fishery Products Hazards and Controls guidance helps processors identify hazards associated with their products and develop control strategies for their HACCP plans. FDA also describes it as a tool for regulatory officials evaluating those plans. For importers, it provides a framework for discussing seafood handling with the processor and the business arranging storage.

FDA explicitly states that the guidance reflects its current thinking and does not bind the agency or the public. An alternative approach may be used if it satisfies applicable statutes and regulations. Keep that distinction clear: recommendations in a guidance document and requirements in a regulation have different roles.

As a practical preparation step, ask the processor for the handling and receiving information relevant to the shipment. Identify the species, product form, packaging, and intended refrigerated or frozen condition. Use those details to discuss which controls apply, rather than assuming that a general request for cold storage fully describes the product's needs.

Match hazard controls to the product and packaging

FDA's reference covers a range of seafood hazards, including scombrotoxin, also called histamine, and bacterial growth and toxin formation associated with time and temperature abuse. Its training for primary processors discusses internal temperature monitoring, sensory examinations, and histamine testing considerations at receiving. These topics support a product-specific discussion; they should not be treated as a universal checklist for every seafood shipment.

Packaging also belongs in that discussion. FDA's training on time-temperature indicators addresses growth and toxin production by Clostridium botulinum in refrigerated seafood packaged with reduced oxygen. The agency also discusses multiple barriers or freezing as control approaches in that context.

For storage planning, describe the packaging and the product's intended condition in the handling instructions. Ask the processor to identify the applicable temperature controls and any restrictions on changing that condition. Treat refrigerated and frozen storage as separate operating specifications, and resolve unclear instructions before booking receiving.

Turn receiving controls into written instructions

According to FDA, its secondary-processor training addresses receiving and storage controls for scombrotoxin formation and pathogen growth. The material also covers continuous temperature monitoring, accuracy checks, and calibration of temperature-recording equipment. These are useful subjects to address when discussing how a shipment will be received and stored.

Prepare a receiving instruction sheet based on the controls relevant to the product. As an operational planning step, include:

  • The product description and its required refrigerated or frozen condition.
  • The temperature limits and monitoring instructions supplied by the responsible processor.
  • The records that should accompany the shipment and the person responsible for reviewing them.
  • The contact who can decide what to do if the shipment condition or records do not match the instructions.

FDA's primary-processor training also discusses harvest vessel records as a strategy for controlling scombrotoxin formation. The agency says its sample records are teaching aids that should be tailored to the fishery and harvesting operation. If those records form part of the processor's control strategy, ask how the relevant documentation will accompany the goods.

Organize prior notice separately from storage

According to FDA's Prior Notice of Imported Foods reference, federal law requires FDA to receive advance notification of food imported or offered for import into the United States. The agency explains that this notice helps it target import inspections with support from U.S. Customs and Border Protection.

FDA also states that a person submitting prior notice must report the name of any country to which the article has been refused entry. Before shipment, identify who will submit the notice and make sure that person receives any applicable refusal information. Include that responsibility in the shipment instructions alongside the receiving arrangements.

For food arriving by international mail, FDA describes requirements beginning October 1, 2026, and an enforcement policy under which it does not expect submission of the mail service name and tracking number until May 1, 2027. That statement concerns international mail. If that shipping method applies, review the specific FDA instructions when organizing the submission.

A practical shipment file should identify the prior notice contact separately from the storage contact, even if the same person coordinates both tasks.

Prepare a cold storage brief before requesting service

The FDA material connects seafood safety with product identification, hazard analysis, receiving controls, and storage controls. Translate those subjects into a short service brief that a storage provider can review. Describe the goods and their handling needs in enough detail to support a concrete discussion.

Include the product and packaging descriptions, intended storage condition, applicable handling instructions, expected arrival arrangements, and planned outbound distribution. Ask how receiving observations and temperature records would be documented, who would review an exception, and how the responsible person would be contacted.

Keep this brief aligned with the processor's relevant control instructions. A storage inquiry should make the operational requirements visible before goods arrive. You can use the contact page to begin that discussion and provide the product information needed to assess the requested service.
