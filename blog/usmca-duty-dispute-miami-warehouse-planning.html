USMCA Duty Dispute Puts Miami 3PL Inventory Plans in Focus

Proposed duty relief for foreign-trade zone manufacturers gives Miami logistics buyers a reason to review sourcing assumptions and warehouse requirements.

The National Association of Foreign-Trade Zones is pressing Congress to address duty disadvantages affecting American manufacturers exporting to Mexico and Canada, FreightWaves reported. The dispute concerns imported components used in goods manufactured in U.S. foreign-trade zones. For businesses planning inventory and distribution through Miami, the practical issue is whether warehouse decisions depend on tariff relief that remains proposed.

Where the Duty Dispute Starts

According to FreightWaves, manufacturers operating in U.S. foreign-trade zones can be required to pay duties on imported components used in finished goods subsequently exported to Mexico or Canada. That obligation can apply even when the finished products qualify for preferential treatment under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, or USMCA.

Jeff Tafel, president of the National Association of Foreign-Trade Zones, told FreightWaves that competing manufacturers in Mexico and Canada have access to duty-relief programs that can eliminate comparable costs. His association is seeking a legislative change to address that difference.

The scope matters for readers buying warehouse services. This is a report about particular manufacturing and export arrangements within foreign-trade zones. It should not be treated as a broad announcement that every importer, exporter or warehouse customer will receive a tariff benefit.

Start by identifying whether the reported arrangement resembles the business's own product flow. List the imported components, where manufacturing occurs and the destination of the finished goods. Use those details when asking the client's licensed customs broker to review the duty assumptions behind a distribution plan. Avoid building a warehouse budget around a benefit described only in general terms.

What the Proposed Legislation Would Change

FreightWaves reported that the association supports companion measures introduced by Rep. Lance Gooden and Sen. Tim Scott. The proposals would provide duty relief for qualifying merchandise manufactured or altered within U.S. foreign-trade zones and subsequently exported to Mexico or Canada. Tafel urged Congress to address the issue without waiting for the USMCA review to conclude.

The report presents these measures as proposed legislation. It does not establish that the requested relief has taken effect. A business considering a different production location or distribution arrangement should keep that distinction visible in its planning documents.

A useful approach is to maintain a working plan based on the duty treatment the business currently uses, alongside a conditional plan for the proposed relief. Label the assumptions clearly. Ask what would change in receiving schedules, inventory allocation and outbound destinations if the business later revises its sourcing strategy.

This gives warehouse discussions a concrete starting point. Instead of requesting space for an undefined future shift, describe the current requirement and the circumstances that would trigger a revision. Any decision dependent on the proposals should remain explicitly conditional until the relevant treatment has been confirmed for the merchandise.

Understand the Benefits and Limits Described in the Report

FreightWaves describes foreign-trade zones as designated areas under U.S. Customs and Border Protection supervision where companies can store, assemble, manufacture and distribute merchandise under special customs procedures. According to the report, the program allows businesses to defer duties on imported goods until they enter U.S. commerce, while certain exports can qualify for duty exemptions.

Those benefits come with qualifications in the same story. Tafel said some recent tariff actions require imported merchandise to enter zones under a status that can limit traditional duty advantages. He also said zones continue to offer benefits through duty deferral, streamlined customs procedures and inventory management efficiencies.

For a logistics buyer, the important reading is conditional: the article describes potential benefits tied to particular circumstances. It does not provide a duty calculation for an individual shipment. Before comparing warehouse proposals, ask the client's licensed customs broker to review the customs assumptions separately from the receiving, storage and fulfillment requirements.

Keep the operational brief specific. Identify what arrives, what handling is requested and where orders will go. That brief can support a discussion about warehouse services without treating a general description of foreign-trade zones as confirmation of a particular customs outcome.

Turn Trade Uncertainty Into Specific Inventory Decisions

Tafel told FreightWaves that companies are evaluating sourcing decisions, freight volumes, warehousing needs, inventory management and transportation patterns. Some are also considering whether to relocate manufacturing or distribution operations across the border. He cautioned that these discussions do not necessarily mean companies have already changed their operations.

That distinction is useful when deciding what to communicate to a warehouse. An internal discussion about a possible move is different from a confirmed receiving requirement. Give the logistics team a clear view of which purchases and shipments are committed and which remain under consideration.

  • Identify the inventory assigned to U.S. customers and the inventory intended for export.
  • Describe any proposed change in where goods originate or where finished orders will be delivered.
  • Specify whether the anticipated work involves pallet storage, individual order fulfillment, wholesale distribution or another handling requirement.
  • State which receiving expectations depend on an unresolved sourcing or manufacturing decision.

Use this review to revise the operating brief before requesting additional space or changing the distribution setup. The aim is to make uncertainty explicit, rather than turn every possible trade-policy outcome into an immediate inventory commitment. Update the brief when the business makes a decision that changes the work requested from its warehouse provider.
